## Quick summary

- Right to Choose has not disappeared. Patients in England still have legal rights around choice for many first outpatient referrals, including some ADHD and autism assessment pathways where the referral is clinically appropriate.
- What has changed is the practical environment around it: local ICB rules, provider capacity, annual activity plans and funding constraints now matter much more.
- Some local NHS systems are warning patients that switching provider does not always mean being seen sooner, because several providers now have their own waiting lists or activity limits.
- The issue is no longer simply “can I use Right to Choose?” but “does this specific provider, in this specific local system, still have capacity and a complete pathway?”
- For families and adults, the key is to understand the route, the provider, the report quality and what happens after assessment — not just the headline waiting time.

Right to Choose is still one of the most searched, shared and misunderstood phrases in ADHD and autism assessment in England.

For many people, it has become shorthand for hope: a possible way out of a long local NHS waiting list. For some families and adults, it has made a real difference. It has allowed them to access assessment through an NHS-contracted provider outside their immediate local service.

But in 2026, the picture is no longer as simple as “ask your GP, choose a provider, get seen faster”.

The legal right has not disappeared. What is changing is the practical system around it. Local NHS commissioners are under pressure, providers are receiving high volumes of referrals, and some areas are now publishing clearer restrictions, warnings or capacity limits.

This matters because a right can exist on paper while becoming harder to use in practice.

## The sources behind this piece

This article draws on NHS England patient choice guidance, the NHS Choice Framework, NHS England’s 2026/27 ADHD and autism payment guidance, local ICB information on Right to Choose, and recent examples from areas including Hampshire and Isle of Wight, Thames Valley, South West London, Black Country and Nottinghamshire.

## 1) Right to Choose still exists — but it is not unlimited

The first thing to say clearly is that Right to Choose has not been abolished.

Under NHS patient choice rules in England, patients generally have the right to choose a provider for a first outpatient appointment, as long as the referral is clinically appropriate and the chosen provider meets NHS requirements for that service.

For ADHD and autism, this has often meant that people referred by their GP could ask to be referred to a provider outside their local NHS pathway, provided that provider held a relevant NHS contract.

That has created a lot of public interest, especially in areas where local waits are extremely long.

But Right to Choose has never meant “any provider, at any time, with guaranteed fast access”. It depends on clinical appropriateness, provider eligibility, contractual arrangements and capacity.

That final word — capacity — is now becoming central.

### Nova take

Right to Choose is still real. But in 2026, the practical question is less about whether the right exists, and more about whether the system behind that right can actually deliver.

## 2) Local ICBs are becoming more visible in how access works

One of the clearest changes is that local ICBs are publishing more detailed information about ADHD and autism assessment routes.

Some pages now explain which providers can be used, how referrals are handled, whether switching provider may affect wait time, and whether there are limits on the number of referrals a provider can accept.

This is important because many people still think of Right to Choose as a national route that works the same everywhere. In practice, the experience can vary significantly.

Some areas appear to be operating with provider activity limits or indicative activity plans. These are not always presented to the public in plain language, but the practical meaning is simple: a provider may be eligible to receive referrals, while still having only a limited amount of funded activity available.

That can lead to a frustrating situation for families. They may be told they have a choice, but then discover that the provider they wanted is not accepting referrals, has a long wait, or is subject to local restrictions.

### Nova take

The national right matters, but the local pathway determines what families actually experience.

## 3) The “fast route” narrative is becoming less reliable

For a while, much of the online conversation around Right to Choose focused on speed.

That made sense. If a local pathway had a multi-year wait and a Right to Choose provider could see someone sooner, the appeal was obvious.

But the more people use a route, the more pressure that route faces. Several providers that were once perceived as faster now have substantial waiting lists of their own. Some local NHS systems are also warning that changing provider may not necessarily shorten the wait.

This does not mean Right to Choose is pointless. It means families and adults need a more careful understanding of what they are choosing.

A shorter wait is useful only if the assessment pathway is appropriate, the report is sufficiently detailed, and the outcome will be understood by the people who need to use it afterwards — whether that is school, university, workplace support, or health professionals.

### Nova take

Speed matters, but speed alone is not the measure of a good assessment pathway.

## 4) NHS England’s 2026/27 payment guidance signals a more structured system

Another important development is NHS England’s 2026/27 ADHD and autism payment guidance.

This may sound technical, but it matters. Payment guidance is not just about how much services are paid. It also helps define what activity is being recognised, counted and commissioned.

For ADHD and autism pathways, that points towards a more structured commissioning environment. The system is trying to clarify what counts as assessment activity, how demand should be managed, and how providers fit into the wider NHS picture.

This is where the discussion becomes more serious than “public vs private” or “local NHS vs Right to Choose”. The underlying question is about pathway quality.

A credible assessment pathway usually needs more than a brief appointment. It should gather developmental history, current presentation, information across settings where relevant, clinical judgement, diagnostic criteria, feedback and a clear report.

As commissioning becomes more structured, the quality and completeness of pathways may become even more important.

### Nova take

The future of ADHD and autism assessment is unlikely to be about access alone. It will also be about whether pathways are complete, credible and clinically useful.

## 5) Families are being asked to navigate a system that is hard to understand

The biggest problem for families and adults is not only delay. It is opacity.

People are often expected to understand the difference between:

- a local NHS neurodevelopmental pathway
- a Right to Choose referral
- a private assessment
- a provider with an NHS contract
- a provider with limited capacity
- an assessment-only route
- an assessment plus post-diagnostic support route

These distinctions matter, but they are rarely explained in a simple way.

That creates anxiety. Families may worry about making the “wrong” choice. Adults may not know whether a report will be recognised. GPs may face requests for referrals into pathways that are changing faster than public guidance can keep up.

The result is a system where the person seeking help often has to become their own pathway manager.

### Nova take

A good pathway should reduce uncertainty, not add another layer of administration for families to decode.

## What this means in practice

For families and adults considering Right to Choose in 2026, the useful questions are now more specific:

- Is the provider currently accepting referrals?
- Does the provider have a relevant NHS contract?
- Is there an activity limit or local restriction affecting referrals?
- What is the estimated wait, and when was that estimate last updated?
- What does the assessment include?
- Is developmental history gathered properly?
- Is information from school, family or an informant included where relevant?
- Is there a feedback session?
- Will the report be detailed enough for the person’s needs?
- What happens after the assessment?

The headline question — “Can I use Right to Choose?” — is still important. But it is no longer enough on its own.

## FAQs

### Has Right to Choose ended for ADHD or autism assessments?

No. Right to Choose has not ended. But access depends on clinical appropriateness, provider eligibility, local arrangements and capacity.

### Does Right to Choose guarantee a shorter wait?

No. It may reduce the wait in some cases, but some providers now have long waits or activity limits of their own.

### Can someone choose any private provider?

No. For Right to Choose, the provider must meet NHS requirements and hold a relevant NHS contract for the service.

### Why are ICBs changing how they explain Right to Choose?

Demand has increased significantly, and local systems are trying to manage capacity, funding and provider activity more tightly.

### What should families be careful about?

Do not choose a pathway based only on speed. Check what the assessment includes, what the report provides, and whether the pathway is suitable for the person’s needs.
