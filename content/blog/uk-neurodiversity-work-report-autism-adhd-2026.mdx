The UK's new neurodiversity at work report makes a strong case for support based on what a person needs, rather than waiting for a diagnostic label. It also recommends a two-week deadline for employers to respond to reasonable-adjustment requests.

But the report does **not** create that deadline, replace the Equality Act or introduce a new right to workplace support.

Published by the Department for Work and Pensions on **2 October 2026**, the [Expert Academic Panel on Neurodiversity's final report](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/expert-academic-panel-on-neurodiversity-final-report-and-recommendations/expert-academic-panel-on-neurodiversity-final-report-and-recommendations) sets out recommendations for government and employers. A separate [government response](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/expert-academic-panel-on-neurodiversity-final-report-and-recommendations/government-response-to-the-expert-academic-panel-on-neurodiversity-report) explains which ideas are already being explored, which connect to existing reforms and which have not been adopted.

That distinction matters. The report points towards a more practical, less diagnosis-dependent workplace system. For employees, however, the most useful question is not simply what the panel recommended. It is what can be relied on now.

## What did the panel recommend?

The independent panel reviewed evidence on recruitment, workplace adjustments, skills, career development and staying in work. Its scope extended beyond autism and ADHD to other developmental and acquired forms of neurodivergence.

Its central recommendations are built around two ideas:

- support should be **needs-led**, responding to the barriers a person encounters in a particular role and environment;
- workplaces should use **Universal Design**, making ordinary systems more accessible from the outset instead of relying only on individual, retrospective adjustments.

The report also recommends changes including:

- a two-week deadline for employers to respond to reasonable-adjustment requests;
- reform of the Disability Confident scheme;
- more resources and changes for Access to Work;
- mandatory disability employment and pay-gap reporting;
- stronger research and data on neurodivergent people's employment outcomes;
- more inclusive recruitment, communication, workplace design and career-development practices.

These are not all at the same stage. Some are recommendations only. Some overlap with work already under way. Others would require further policy development or legislation.

> A recommendation in a government-published report is not automatically a new legal duty. The practical test is what the government has accepted, what it is still exploring and what has actually come into force.

## What does “needs-led” support mean?

A needs-led approach starts with the interaction between the person, the task and the workplace.

Instead of assuming that every autistic employee needs one set of adjustments, or that every person with ADHD needs another, it asks:

- What part of the job is creating a barrier?
- In what situations does the difficulty arise?
- What change could reduce that disadvantage?
- Is the support still effective when the role or environment changes?

This matters because people with the same diagnosis can have very different working patterns, strengths and support needs. It also matters for people who are waiting for an assessment, do not have access to one or do not identify with a single diagnostic category.

The panel argues that diagnosis-led systems can widen inequality when people with clearer pathways or greater access to assessment receive support while others with comparable needs do not.

That does not make diagnosis irrelevant. An autism or ADHD assessment answers a clinical question and may help someone understand their experiences, consider treatment or access services that require clinical evidence. Workplace support answers a different question: what would help this person work safely and effectively in this setting?

Acas already advises employers to offer support for neurodivergence whether or not a worker has a diagnosis. A worker does not need a diagnosis to be considered disabled under the Equality Act 2010, although whether the Act applies depends on the individual's circumstances.

## What is Universal Design at work?

Universal Design means anticipating human variation when a workplace process is created. The aim is to reduce avoidable barriers before someone has to disclose a condition or request a special exception.

The report's examples include:

- providing interview questions in more than one format;
- using accessible shared systems for tasks and priorities;
- offering remote working or adapted input methods as standard options where the role allows;
- providing captioning and screen-reader-compatible documents as a baseline;
- designing communication, policies and physical environments with different sensory, processing and access needs in mind.

This is not the same as treating everyone identically. A universally designed process should be easier for more people to use, but some employees will still need individual reasonable adjustments.

For example, a meeting agenda sent in advance may help many staff. One employee might still need written follow-up after the meeting, permission to keep their camera off or additional processing time. Universal Design reduces unnecessary friction; it does not remove the need for personalised support.

## Has the government adopted the two-week adjustment deadline?

No.

The panel recommends requiring employers to respond to reasonable-adjustment requests within two weeks. The government's response acknowledges the concern about delays and refers to research published in September 2026 and ongoing work through the Keep Britain Working programme. It does not announce a new two-week legal deadline.

Employees should therefore not treat the recommendation as if it were already law. Employers should still deal with requests promptly and communicate clearly, but the panel's proposed time limit is not a new statutory rule.

This point is especially important because a response is not necessarily the same as full implementation. Some adjustments can be agreed quickly; others may require occupational-health input, equipment, funding or changes involving several teams. A clear acknowledgement, named owner, next step and review date can prevent a request from disappearing into an undefined process.

Nova's existing guide to [reasonable adjustments for autistic and ADHD employees](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/reasonable-adjustments-autism-adhd-work-uk-2026) explains how to describe a workplace barrier, make a specific request and keep a written record.

## What is the government doing now?

The response links the panel's recommendations to several existing or developing programmes.

### A 16-week Universal Design exercise

The government says it has started a 16-week policy-discovery exercise on how workplace design principles could apply to physical environments, policies, procedures and communication. This is exploratory work. It is not a new workplace standard or legal entitlement.

### Disability Confident reform

The government says it is reforming the Disability Confident scheme, including stronger criteria, verification and limits on remaining at entry level. This responds to concerns that participation in the scheme has not always demonstrated meaningful employment outcomes.

### More Access to Work staff

The response says Access to Work staffing is being increased by more than 70% to address the backlog, while a wider reform programme is still being developed. The panel's broader recommendations — including removing the support cap and making awards more portable — should not be read as decisions already implemented.

Access to Work remains separate from an employer's duty to make reasonable adjustments. It can fund eligible practical support above and beyond what it is reasonable for an employer to provide. Current government guidance says a formal diagnosis is not required to apply.

### Employment and reporting reforms

The government points to Employment Rights Act changes, including an increase in the time limit for certain Employment Tribunal claims from three to six months from October 2026. It also says mandatory ethnicity and disability pay-gap reporting is planned for employers with 250 or more staff, but legislation will be introduced when parliamentary time allows.

These measures should not be blurred together. One is an enacted change described as taking effect; the reporting proposal is not yet in force.

### What the government has not agreed to do

The response states that there are no plans to make sensory-friendly building design a legislative requirement. It supports existing accessibility standards and says officials are exploring how they can be embedded more widely.

It also does not announce the panel's proposed two-week response deadline or confirm every recommended Access to Work change.

> The direction is clearer than the timetable: government supports needs-led practice and is testing Universal Design, but several of the panel's most concrete proposals remain unimplemented.

## What can autistic and ADHD employees use now?

You do not need to wait for the policy work to finish before raising a barrier at work.

If support would help, it can be useful to record:

1. the task or situation that is difficult;
2. what happens and how it affects your work;
3. the change you would like to try;
4. any alternative that could achieve the same result;
5. when the arrangement should be reviewed.

A diagnosis can provide useful context, but it is not a substitute for explaining the functional impact. Equally, not having a diagnosis should not prevent an employer from discussing support.

If you are considering a clinical assessment, Nova's guides explain what happens in an [adult autism assessment](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/adult-autism-assessment-what-to-expect), an [adult ADHD assessment](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/adult-adhd-assessment-what-to-expect) and a [combined autism and ADHD assessment](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/adult-combined-autism-adhd-assessment-what-to-expect). Those pathways are separate from asking for workplace support.

## What can employers do without waiting for new rules?

The report gives employers a useful test: examine the ordinary systems that repeatedly make individual adjustments necessary.

That might mean:

- giving candidates clear information about the recruitment process;
- allowing more than one way to receive or respond to information;
- making priorities, ownership and deadlines visible;
- creating a clear route for adjustment requests;
- responding quickly even when a final decision needs more work;
- reviewing adjustments as roles and circumstances change;
- checking recruitment technology for bias or avoidable exclusion;
- involving neurodivergent staff in the design of policies and processes.

These actions do not require an employer to predict every individual's needs. They create a more usable baseline and make personalised conversations easier when they are needed.

## What does the report change in practice?

The report changes the policy conversation more than it changes an employee's rights today.

It gives official weight to an approach many neurodivergent people and workplace specialists have argued for: support should focus on the real barrier, not require someone to fit a diagnostic stereotype, and not begin only after a system has failed them.

The government has accepted that direction and begun work on Universal Design, employer guidance, Disability Confident and Access to Work capacity. But the final report should not be presented as a package of new legal rights.

For autistic and ADHD employees, the immediate message is practical. Describe the barrier, ask for a specific change, agree how the request will be handled and review whether the support works. For employers, the challenge is broader: design ordinary working practices so fewer people have to fight for access one adjustment at a time.
