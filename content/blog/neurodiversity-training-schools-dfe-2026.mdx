A new, free neurodiversity training package for schools and colleges launches in England on **23 September 2026**. Funded by the Department for Education and delivered through Whole School SEND and nasen, it is designed to help school and college leaders train their own staff in more inclusive, neuro-affirming practice.

For families of autistic children, children with ADHD, and children whose needs are still being understood, the important question is not simply that another training resource exists. It is whether it can make conversations with school more practical.

The package will not create a new legal entitlement, replace an assessment or guarantee a particular adjustment. But it gives schools a shared set of materials around areas that often sit at the centre of everyday difficulties: executive function, adaptive teaching, independence and the way different needs can overlap.

## What is launching on 23 September?

Whole School SEND describes the resource as a **“Train the Trainer”** package. Instead of requiring every teacher to attend an external course, a school or college can use the materials internally, led by people who already understand the setting.

There are separate versions for **primary schools, secondary schools and further education**. Each includes ready-made slides, activities, case studies and presenter guidance, with distinct material for leadership teams and teachers.

The package also contains shorter modules on executive function, intersectionality, adaptive teaching and independence.

Whole School SEND says the materials are aligned with current Department for Education and Education Endowment Foundation guidance and are intended to be needs-led, strengths-based and adaptable to the setting.

The first free introductory webinar takes place on **22 September 2026**, the day before the resources launch. Further sessions are scheduled for December 2026 and February 2027.

## Why this could matter for autistic and ADHD pupils

Neurodivergent pupils do not all need the same support. Two children with the same diagnosis can have very different strengths, barriers and ways of communicating or learning. Equally, a child can need meaningful support before any diagnostic assessment has taken place.

That is why the most useful part of this launch may be its focus on everyday teaching rather than diagnosis-specific checklists.

### Executive function

Executive functions include skills involved in planning, starting tasks, holding information in mind, shifting between activities and organising actions towards a goal. Difficulties in these areas can affect autistic pupils and pupils with ADHD, although they vary considerably from one child to another.

In school, an executive-function difficulty can sometimes be mistaken for a lack of effort. A pupil may understand the lesson but struggle to begin a multi-step task, remember several verbal instructions, organise equipment or move from one activity to another.

Training that helps staff ask *what is making this task difficult?* rather than simply *why has this pupil not done it?* can lead to more useful support.

That support might include breaking instructions into smaller steps, providing a visual sequence, reducing unnecessary working-memory demands or making transitions more predictable. The right approach depends on the individual pupil.

### Adaptive teaching

Adaptive teaching is about adjusting teaching so that pupils can access learning without lowering expectations unnecessarily.

For a neurodivergent pupil, that could mean changing how information is presented, allowing a different way to demonstrate understanding, making instructions more explicit or adjusting the environment where sensory or attentional demands are creating a barrier.

The important distinction is that inclusion does not necessarily mean every pupil doing exactly the same thing in exactly the same way.

### Independence

Support should help a child participate and develop skills, not simply make them dependent on an adult being beside them throughout the school day.

A thoughtful approach to independence asks what scaffolding is useful now, what the pupil can already do, and how support can be adjusted as skills and confidence develop.

This can be particularly important for children who appear to cope because an adult is quietly doing a large amount of planning, prompting or regulation around them.

## What parents can reasonably ask their child’s school

Families do not need to approach this new package as something they must persuade a school to adopt immediately. It may be more useful as a starting point for specific conversations.

If your child is struggling in school, questions such as these can help move the discussion from labels to needs:

- Which parts of the school day seem to create the biggest barriers?
- Are difficulties with starting, organising or switching tasks being considered as possible executive-function needs?
- What adaptations have been tried, and what happened when they were used consistently?
- Are strategies shared between staff, or does support depend on which teacher is present?
- How is the school balancing support with opportunities for independence?
- How are your child’s own views being included when adjustments are reviewed?

From 23 September, families can also ask whether the school’s SENCO or inclusion lead is aware of the new Whole School SEND neurodiversity package and whether any of its materials could be useful for staff development.

That is a more constructive question than assuming the existence of the resource means a school is obliged to deliver every part of it.

## Does a child need an autism or ADHD diagnosis before school can make changes?

A diagnosis can be valuable. It can help a child and family understand a pattern of needs, inform recommendations and make it easier to communicate a profile across settings.

But schools should not treat diagnosis as the point at which all support begins.

The new training package itself is framed around inclusive, needs-led practice rather than waiting for a particular diagnostic label. That fits with the broader direction of SEND support in England, including the current emphasis on identifying and responding to needs earlier.

If a child is having difficulty accessing learning, communicating, managing the sensory environment, organising work or participating in school life, those barriers can be explored while an assessment is being considered or while a child is waiting.

## What this launch does not change

It is worth being precise about the limits of the announcement.

The training package is a **professional-development resource**. Its launch does not, by itself:

- change the diagnostic criteria for autism or ADHD
- create a new NHS assessment pathway
- change EHCP legislation
- guarantee a specific adjustment for every neurodivergent pupil
- mean that every school will implement the materials in exactly the same way
- replace individual assessment of a child’s strengths and needs

This matters because families are already navigating several SEND changes in England this year. A new training resource should not be confused with a change in statutory rights or with the separate rollout of services such as Experts at Hand.

Its potential value is more practical: giving education staff a structured, free way to improve shared understanding and translate that understanding into classroom practice.

## If your child is currently waiting for an assessment

Waiting for an autism or ADHD assessment can leave families feeling as though everything else is on hold. School support does not need to work that way.

While you are waiting, it can help to keep a short record of the situations that are difficult, the strategies that have been tried and the changes that genuinely help. Concrete examples are usually more useful than broad descriptions such as “struggles with attention” or “finds school overwhelming”.

For example, note whether written instructions reduce repeated prompting, whether a predictable transition helps at the end of lessons, or whether a quieter workspace changes how much work your child can complete.

Those observations can support conversations with school now and can also provide useful context later if your child has a neurodevelopmental assessment.

## A useful resource — if it changes practice

The strongest feature of the new package is that it is designed to be used inside real schools and colleges, rather than remaining specialist knowledge held by one person.

Whether it makes a difference will depend on implementation: staff need time to engage with it, leaders need to make inclusive practice consistent, and individual pupils still need to be listened to.

For families, the launch creates one more practical tool for conversations with school. From this week, there is a free, nationally available set of neurodiversity training materials that schools can draw on. The useful next question is not simply whether staff have completed training, but **what has changed for the pupil as a result**.
