If autism runs in families, does an autistic child's diagnosis mean a parent is autistic too? And if genes are involved, why is there no simple DNA test for autism?

On **15 September 2026**, the National Autistic Society published a new statement about autism's genetic basis, drawing on its review of **83 research papers**. The announcement brings those questions into focus. It is a new explanation of an existing body of evidence, not the discovery of a single cause or a new diagnostic test. [Read the NAS announcement](https://www.autism.org.uk/what-we-do/news/autism-is-genetic).

The useful distinction is between explaining patterns across families and being able to predict or diagnose autism in one person.

## What does it mean to say autism is genetic?

Genetic differences make a substantial contribution to the likelihood of being autistic. But the genetics are varied: there is no single genetic pattern shared by every autistic person.

A [research review by Havdahl and colleagues](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/psychological-medicine/article/genetic-contributions-to-autism-spectrum-disorder/89240047F6928249D9DE91A6A6CFBD52) describes several contributions:

- **Common genetic variants:** DNA differences found widely in the population. Individually, their effects are small; many can contribute together. This is called a polygenic contribution.
- **Rare variants:** less common differences that can have larger effects, although their presence does not always lead to the same outcome.
- **De novo variants:** differences that arise newly, rather than simply following an established family pattern. Some occur in an egg or sperm or during early development.

These are not three mutually exclusive types of autistic person. Different contributions can coexist. “Genetic” is therefore broader than “inherited from an autistic parent”.

> A strong genetic contribution does not mean a simple inheritance pattern, or a predictable outcome for every family.

## What does the research actually measure?

One influential study, published in **2019**, used records from more than two million people across five countries. It estimated autism's heritability at approximately **80%**. That is an estimate from a statistical model of population differences, not a percentage measured inside an individual. [Bai and colleagues, JAMA Psychiatry](https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamapsychiatry/fullarticle/2737582).

Heritability describes how much variation in a population's underlying likelihood of autism is attributable to inherited genetic differences under the model used. It does **not** mean:

- a particular person is “80% genetically autistic”;
- an autistic parent has an 80% chance of having an autistic child;
- scientists can explain 80% of one person's experiences from their DNA.

The study's estimates varied between countries and models. Its authors also identified limitations, including assumptions about how genetic and environmental influences relate to each other. It supports a substantial inherited contribution without providing a personalised family forecast.

## Does this mean autism has nothing to do with other influences?

No. A strong genetic contribution is not the same as a complete explanation of development.

The [NHS England genomics resource](https://www.genomicseducation.hee.nhs.uk/genotes/knowledge-hub/autism-spectrum-disorder/) describes autism's genetics as complex, with potential environmental contributions. In research, “environmental” is a broad technical category, not shorthand for parenting.

Researchers must also distinguish an association from a cause. The Havdahl review explains that apparent links between exposures and autism can be affected by genetic and other differences between the groups being studied. Observing a link does not settle what produced it.

The sensible conclusion is neither “we know every cause” nor “genes do not matter”. There is strong evidence for genetic contributions, with important questions about how different influences work together.

## If my child is autistic, does that mean I am?

Not necessarily. The [NAS family guidance](https://www.autism.org.uk/advice-and-guidance/about-autism/what-causes-autism) explains that an autistic child can have non-autistic parents. Relatives may share traits without all meeting diagnostic criteria. Having autistic relatives increases the likelihood of autism, but does not make it inevitable.

For a parent, a child's assessment may prompt useful reflection: perhaps sensory experiences, communication differences or the need for routine feel familiar. That can be a reason to explore your own history, not a diagnosis in itself.

Equally, the absence of a recognised family history does not rule autism out. The varied genetic pathways matter here; a family tree is not a diagnostic test.

There is also no useful basis for turning inheritance into blame. Explaining a family pattern and deciding that somebody caused a problem are very different things. The NAS explicitly addresses this concern in its guidance.

## Can a genetic test diagnose autism?

There is no routine genetic test that can establish or exclude autism by itself. But saying “there is no test for autism” should not be taken to mean genetic investigations are never relevant to an autistic person.

Two different clinical questions are involved.

**An autism assessment asks whether someone's development and experiences meet the criteria for autism.** The NHS describes assessments that bring together developmental history, information from the person and people who know them, and observations or other assessment work. [NHS autism assessments](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/autism/assessments/).

**Genetic investigations ask whether there is an identifiable genetic condition that helps explain a wider clinical picture.** NHS England's [guidance for children with suspected autism](https://www.genomicseducation.hee.nhs.uk/genotes/in-the-clinic/presentation-child-with-suspected-autism-spectrum-disorder/) highlights additional features such as moderate to profound intellectual disability, motor developmental delay, seizures or congenital anomalies when considering testing. Eligibility depends on the clinical situation and current testing criteria; autism alone does not automatically qualify someone.

The NHS England knowledge resource also stresses that someone with a genetic syndrome and autistic features should still be assessed for autism. Identifying a syndrome does not replace that assessment.

> Genetic investigations and an autism assessment can complement each other. They answer different questions.

## What can families usefully ask?

If genetic testing has been suggested, you could ask:

- What specific question would this test help answer?
- Is it being considered because of additional developmental or medical features?
- What would a finding change about care or follow-up?
- What would an inconclusive result mean?
- Would genetic counselling help us understand the implications for relatives?

These are conversation prompts, not a checklist for deciding eligibility yourself. NHS England's testing guidance applies to England; arrangements in Scotland, Wales and Northern Ireland differ.

The [NHS guide to genetic testing](https://www.nhs.uk/tests-and-treatments/genetic-and-genomic-testing/) explains that results can identify a relevant change, find no known relevant change, or leave uncertainty. Genetic counselling can help people understand possible results and their implications before and after testing. Testing is not something a family has to navigate through a consumer DNA report.

If your question is instead whether you or your child might be autistic, our guides explain the separate process of [child autism assessment](/blog/child-autism-assessment-what-to-expect) and [adult autism assessment](/blog/adult-autism-assessment-what-to-expect).

The value of this evidence is a clearer explanation of why autism can appear across generations, without promising certainty about one person. It can inform questions about family history and appropriate investigations. Understanding someone's communication, sensory experiences, strengths and support needs still requires attention to that person.
