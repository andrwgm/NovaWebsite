On 29 October 2026, the Department for Education confirmed that most local areas in England now have an approved plan for improving special educational needs and disabilities (SEND) services. The government also published allocations for a grant intended to address historic deficits in councils' education budgets.

For parents of autistic children, children with ADHD and young people with other special educational needs, the important question is not simply whether a plan has been approved. It is **what the local partnership has committed to doing, when those changes will happen and how families will know whether support is improving**.

There is an equally important limit to the announcement: approval does not mean a service has already improved, and the new grant is not a pot of money that schools can immediately spend on extra support.

## What was announced on 29 October?

In a [written statement to Parliament](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-10-29/hcws431), the minister for school standards confirmed that:

- **139 local SEND reform plans were approved on first submission.**
- **12 plans were initially deferred** for revision. Two of those areas — **Cumberland and Liverpool** — have since submitted revised plans that were approved.
- **10 areas remain in the deferred group** and are expected to resubmit by **30 November 2026**.
- Approval is a condition for receiving an allocation from the **High Needs Stability Grant**, which covers 90% of eligible historic Dedicated Schools Grant (DSG) deficits.
- Approved areas will be subject to **quarterly progress reviews**. A national performance and delivery framework is due to be published later in 2026.

In other words, **141 local partnerships have approved plans as of the statement**, but that number includes two approved following revision. The statement does not say that all 141 areas are already delivering the improvements described in their plans.

The plans are a joint responsibility of local authorities and partners in education and health, including NHS integrated care boards. That matters because families often experience SEND support as a series of disconnected services rather than a single system.

## Which areas are still waiting for approval?

As listed in the 29 October parliamentary statement, the 10 local areas whose plans remain deferred are:

- Bradford
- Cambridgeshire
- Lewisham
- Milton Keynes
- Newham
- Redbridge
- Slough
- Southend-on-Sea
- Thurrock
- West Berkshire

**A deferred plan is not the same as a failed SEND service**, just as an approved plan is not proof that a local service is working well. The government says the deferred areas are receiving additional support to revise their proposals.

If your council is not in that list, you can check its status in the [full parliamentary statement](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-10-29/hcws431), which names the approved areas and their NHS partners. The lists describe the position at the time of the announcement; they may change as revised plans are assessed.

## What does the High Needs Stability Grant actually pay for?

The funding announcement can easily be misunderstood.

The [Department for Education's 29 October grant publication](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/high-needs-stability-grant-2026-to-2027) sets out allocations and conditions for councils with eligible DSG deficits. It is designed to cover **90% of qualifying deficits accumulated up to 31 March 2026**, provided the council's local SEND reform plan is approved and other grant conditions are met.

The government's [explanatory note](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/explanatory-note-on-the-governments-approach-to-dedicated-schools-grant-deficits/explanatory-note-on-the-governments-approach-dedicated-schools-grant-deficits) is explicit that this grant must be used to reduce the historic DSG deficit, subject to its accounting rules. It is **not additional funding that can simply be allocated to a new teaching assistant, an autism assessment appointment or an individual child's therapy**.

Councils are expected to meet the remaining eligible historic deficit from their own resources in 2028/29. The government has said that the grant is conditional on the local reform plan, not that an approved plan automatically resolves ongoing pressure on SEND services.

This distinction is especially relevant following recent forecasts about future SEND deficits. **Clearing much of the debt built up before April 2026 does not, by itself, establish that future demand or spending pressures have been solved.**

## Does an approved plan change your child's EHCP rights?

**No new change to an individual EHCP's legal status was announced on 29 October.**

Under [section 42 of the Children and Families Act 2014](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2014/6/section/42), where a local authority maintains an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP), it must secure the special educational provision specified in that plan, subject to the statutory qualifications. This is usually the provision described in **Section F**.

An approved local reform plan does not replace an EHCP. Nor does a deferred plan suspend a council's existing statutory responsibilities. The government has not announced that families must wait for a local reform plan to be implemented before the provision in an existing EHCP is arranged.

For a child without an EHCP, school-based SEN support is a separate matter. Schools should consider identified special educational needs rather than wait for an autism or ADHD diagnosis before discussing appropriate support. The [government's SEN support guidance](https://www.gov.uk/children-with-special-educational-needs/special-educational-needs-support) explains the existing school support framework.

## What could change in practice — and what has not happened yet?

The government is asking local partnerships to move from planning to delivery. It has promised regular reviews, closer oversight of areas with weaker delivery capacity, and improvement notices where performance is inadequate.

Those are **accountability arrangements**, not evidence that a particular child will receive an assessment, specialist placement or therapy sooner from next week.

For families, there are three separate things to watch:

1. **The local plan:** what the council, NHS and education partners have actually committed to deliver.
2. **Implementation:** when a new service, staffing arrangement, support offer or referral pathway will start, and who can access it.
3. **Results:** whether families see shorter delays, clearer communication and support that matches children's identified needs.

A partnership can have an approved plan and still have substantial work to do. The DfE explicitly says approval is not an endorsement of current performance.

## Five useful questions to ask your local SEND partnership

You do not need to become an expert in education funding to follow what happens next. If you want to understand what this announcement means where you live, these are reasonable questions for your council's SEND team, local SEND partnership or parent carer forum:

1. **Can I read the approved local SEND reform plan, or a public summary?** If it is not published, ask when and how families will be able to see the commitments.
2. **Which changes affect children with autism or ADHD, including those without a diagnosis?** Ask about practical support in schools, specialist advice and access to assessment pathways.
3. **What is the implementation timetable?** A promise to improve services is different from a service that is open for referrals.
4. **How will progress be measured and reported to families?** Ask whether families and young people will be involved in the quarterly reviews or in local scrutiny.
5. **Who should I contact if support in an existing EHCP is not being provided?** Ask for the responsible team and a clear written response about the missing provision.

If you are concerned about your child's current support, **do not wait for the reform timetable**. Speak to the school SENCO and, where an EHCP is in place, the local authority SEND team about the provision that is needed now. Your local SEND Information, Advice and Support Service (SENDIASS) can offer free, impartial guidance.

## What should families watch for next?

The next confirmed milestone is **30 November 2026**, the date by which the remaining deferred areas are expected to resubmit their plans. The DfE has also said that it will publish its Local Area SEND Performance and Delivery Framework later in 2026 and review local progress quarterly.

These developments will be more informative than plan approval alone. They should make it easier to judge whether a partnership's commitments are translating into better experiences for children and families — although the government has not yet published evidence of outcomes from this new approval process.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does an approved SEND reform plan mean my child will get an assessment sooner?

Not automatically. Approval means the plan met the government's threshold. It does not confirm a shorter individual waiting time or an immediate change to referral arrangements.

### Are the 10 deferred councils losing their children's SEND services?

No such conclusion follows from the statement. Their plans require revision and resubmission. Existing legal duties remain in place.

### Is the 90% grant new money for classroom support?

No. It is specifically intended to reduce eligible historic DSG deficits. It should not be presented as a new per-child budget or a guarantee of additional provision.

### Does this announcement alter Section F of an EHCP?

No. The existing legal duty to secure specified special educational provision remains in force.

### Where can I check my local authority's status?

Use the [29 October parliamentary statement](https://questions-statements.parliament.uk/written-statements/detail/2026-10-29/hcws431), which lists approved and deferred areas. For your area's specific commitments, ask the local SEND partnership for its plan or public summary.
