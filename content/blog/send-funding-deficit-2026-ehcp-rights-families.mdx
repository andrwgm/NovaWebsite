## Why this matters now

A new survey of councils in England forecasts **£10.4 billion in cumulative SEND deficits by March 2028**. For parents of autistic children, children with ADHD and those still waiting for an assessment, that raises an immediate question: does the financial pressure change what schools and councils must provide?

**The forecast does not change a child's existing legal rights.** It is a warning about the sustainability of SEND funding, not a new rule for Education, Health and Care Plans (EHCPs).

## What the new figures actually show

On 13 October 2026, the County Councils Network published findings from its annual SEND deficits survey. Finance officers from **89 local authorities** responded, representing **73% of EHCPs in England**. Their figures were scaled to estimate the position across all 151 upper-tier councils.

The survey projects cumulative deficits of **£4.8bn by March 2027**, potentially reaching **£10.4bn by March 2028**. These are estimates of future financial pressure, not £10.4bn already owed today.

CCN says deficits are building again despite government support intended to clear much of the historic shortfall accumulated up to March 2026.

The figures are significant, but they are **not a measurement of the support received by an individual child**. They do not establish that a particular council has refused provision or that an individual EHCP will change.

Primary sources: [CCN announcement](https://www.countycouncilsnetwork.org.uk/news/councils-not-out-of-the-woods-send-debt-to-hit-record-ps10bn-just-18-months-after-government-bailout) and [survey overview](https://www.countycouncilsnetwork.org.uk/publications/overview-of-the-2026-send-deficits-survey).

## Why are councils under so much pressure?

Department for Education statistics published in June 2026 recorded **718,838 active EHCPs in January 2026**, **12.5% more** than a year earlier.

That figure covers children and young people aged **0 to 25**. It is not the same as the smaller school-census count of pupils with EHCPs.

The DfE also reported that only **46.1% of new plans issued during 2025** met the usual 20-week statutory timescale, excluding cases where exceptions apply. That is a more direct indicator of administrative pressure than the deficit forecast alone.

The survey adds a financial perspective. Council finance officers expect the cost of meeting demand to continue outpacing resources.

There is a difference between a council's budget position and a child's entitlement to support. Financial pressure may affect how services operate in practice, but it does not itself rewrite the rules governing assessments or the provision in an existing plan.

Source: [DfE EHCP statistics 2026](https://explore-education-statistics.service.gov.uk/find-statistics/education-health-and-care-plans/2026).

## Does a SEND deficit change what an EHCP must provide?

**No.** Under **section 42 of the Children and Families Act 2014**, the local authority must secure the special educational provision specified in an EHCP. This is usually the provision described in **Section F**.

The responsibility remains with the local authority even when a school delivers day-to-day support.

A funding dispute between a school and a council does not remove that duty. There are statutory qualifications, including where a family has made suitable alternative arrangements. The wording of the plan matters.

Families should check what the plan actually specifies, rather than rely only on a general assurance that the school is doing its best.

Sources: [Children and Families Act 2014, section 42](https://www.legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2014/6/section/42) and [IPSEA guidance on who must arrange EHCP provision](https://www.ipsea.org.uk/FAQs/is-it-the-responsibility-of-the-nursery-school-or-college-to-arrange-the-provision-specified-in-an-ehc-plan).

### What if your child has no EHCP?

Children do not need an EHCP — or an autism or ADHD diagnosis — before their school considers support for identified special educational needs.

For pupils with SEN, mainstream schools have a duty to use their **best endeavours** to secure the special educational provision their needs call for. This is different from the local authority's duty to secure provision in Section F of an EHCP.

If your child is struggling now, talk to the SENCO about the barriers they face and what the school can put in place. You do not need to wait for a diagnostic label or a national reform.

## If your child's EHCP support is not happening

Financial headlines can make families feel they have little room to ask questions. A clear written record is often the best starting point.

### 1. Check Section F against what happens each week

Identify the support written into the plan: a named intervention, frequency of input or specialist provision. Check whether it is being delivered. If the wording is vague or needs updating, ask for advice about a review or amendment.

### 2. Record the gaps

Keep dates, the support expected and what happened instead. Focus on observable facts rather than trying to establish why the gap occurred.

### 3. Ask the school and local authority different questions

A SENCO may explain how support is timetabled and who should deliver it. But if provision in Section F is not being secured, the **local authority** remains responsible.

Write to the council's SEND team, quote the relevant part of the plan and ask how and when the missing provision will be arranged. Keep copies of correspondence.

### 4. Seek independent advice before escalating

Your local **SEND Information, Advice and Support Service (SENDIASS)** offers free, impartial help. **IPSEA** also provides legal information and template letters.

The appropriate route depends on the issue. Disagreement about the **contents of a final EHCP** is different from a failure to **deliver provision already specified**. Complaints, appeals and legal remedies are not interchangeable; ask for advice on the correct next step.

## What happens next?

The survey does not change SEND law.

The CCN survey is evidence used by councils to argue for greater funding certainty. It is not a change to the law. The government has proposed wider SEND reforms, but proposals, funding decisions and statutory rights are separate matters. Families should follow confirmed decisions affecting their child's provision, not speculation.

Nova has previously explained the [September SEND support changes](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/send-support-september-2026-experts-at-hand-school-funding) and the [DfE-funded school training package](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/neurodiversity-training-schools-dfe-2026). Those articles cover school support; this survey concerns the financial pressures behind the system.

## What should families take away?

The forecast is a serious warning about SEND funding. It is **not a new cap on individual EHCP support**, a new diagnosis requirement or proof that every council is cutting provision.

## Frequently asked questions

### Is the £10.4bn deficit already confirmed?

No. It is a projection for March 2028, not a current debt total.

### Can a council reduce Section F support because budgets are tight?

A funding shortfall alone does not remove the section 42 duty to secure provision.

### Is a diagnosis required for SEN support?

No. Schools should consider a child's identified educational needs and barriers.

### Who can help when EHCP support is missing?

Ask the school and local authority SEND team. SENDIASS and IPSEA provide independent advice.
