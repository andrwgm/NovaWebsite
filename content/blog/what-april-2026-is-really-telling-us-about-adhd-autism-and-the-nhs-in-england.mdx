## Quick summary

- ADHD demand has increased sharply, with sustained pressure on waiting lists across England
- NHS England is beginning to standardise assessment pathways, pricing and commissioning
- SEND reform aims to provide earlier support, not only diagnosis-led access
- Workforce shortages may limit how much actually changes in practice
- The system is shifting direction, but core constraints remain unresolved

---

In April 2026, several major updates across ADHD, autism and SEND policy in England revealed a clearer picture of where the system is heading — and where it is struggling most.

If you are a parent, adult or professional trying to understand what this means in practice, this article breaks down the key changes and what they actually imply.

April 2026 has not produced a single breakthrough. What it has done is make a few things harder to ignore: the scale of demand, the limits of the current system, the government’s push towards earlier support in mainstream settings, and the uncomfortable question of whether the workforce is anywhere near strong enough to make those ambitions real.

## 1) The backlog story is no longer just anecdotal

The most important document shaping discussion this month is the interim independent review into mental health conditions, ADHD and autism in England.

Its clearest signal is on ADHD demand. The review says the number of children and young people waiting for an ADHD assessment rose from around 21,000 in April 2019 to around 270,000 by December 2025. It also notes that referrals, waiting lists and recorded diagnoses have risen sharply, with especially notable growth among adolescent girls and young adult women.

On autism, the report is more cautious, but still important. It points to significant variation across England in the proportion of people referred for an autism assessment who then receive a diagnosis, and says variation by ethnicity needs closer examination in the next phase of the review.

That matters because it suggests the current problem is not just “too much demand”. It is also variation in thresholds, pathways and access.

### Nova take

Families are often pushed into a polarised debate that does not match what services actually look like on the ground. The more accurate picture is messier: unmet need, inconsistent recognition and real distress can all exist at the same time.

## 2) NHS England is starting to standardise how assessment services are commissioned

Under the 2026/27 NHS Payment Scheme, NHS England has published ADHD and autism payment guidance aimed at commissioners and service leads.

This signals a shift away from highly variable local systems towards a more defined framework.

Key changes include:

- avoiding unnecessary reassessments
- greater acceptance of independent diagnostic reports, including Right to Choose pathways
- more structured pre-diagnostic support before medication

## 3) SEND reform is pushing support earlier

The SEND reform consultation proposes more support inside mainstream settings and less reliance on diagnosis as the only gateway.

This includes:

- a stronger Universal offer
- new support layers (Targeted, Targeted Plus, Specialist)
- the “Experts at Hand” model

The broad policy direction is easy to understand. The government is signalling that too much pressure currently builds around formal thresholds, statutory plans and diagnosis-led routes to help. In theory, earlier support inside schools and mainstream settings should mean fewer families reach crisis point before anything meaningful happens.

That is why this part of the April debate matters so much. It is not only about autism or ADHD assessment pathways. It is also about whether the wider system can offer support earlier, more consistently and with less friction.

### Nova take

This only helps if earlier support is genuinely available.  

If not, families may still need diagnosis just to unlock help.

## 4) The workforce problem remains critical

The Education Policy Institute estimates England needs around 1,400 more educational psychologists.

> [English councils need to hire 1,400 more educational psychologists, says report](https://www.theguardian.com/education/2026/apr/16/english-councils-need-to-hire-1400-more-educational-psychologists-says-report)
> 

At the same time, a significant proportion of professionals are considering leaving.

This matters because workforce capacity is not a side issue sitting separately from reform. It sits underneath almost everything else discussed in this post. Earlier support, better school input, more joined-up pathways and less escalation all depend on having enough skilled professionals available to do that work.

Without that capacity, even sensible reforms can struggle to translate into real improvement. The system may set out a better model on paper, but still fail to deliver it consistently in practice.

### Nova take

Workforce is the delivery problem behind the policy problem.  

Without enough professionals, reform risks staying theoretical.

## 5) More focus on coordination and complex cases

NHS England has also introduced minimum standards for keyworking services.

This is a smaller development than the wider ADHD, autism and SEND headlines, but it is still significant. It shows a continued emphasis on coordination, accountability and support around children and young people with more complex needs, especially where there is a higher risk of crisis or hospital admission.

In other words, the policy conversation is not only about how people enter the system through assessment. It is also about what happens around them when needs are more complex, support must be coordinated across services, and families are already under pressure.

### Nova take

This is a reminder that good care is not only about diagnosis.  

It is also about coordination when needs become more complex.

## What this means in practice

### 1. Pressure is now openly acknowledged

National bodies are now describing backlog pressure more openly. That does not create quick fixes, but it does make it harder to treat these issues as isolated local problems.

### 2. Support may become less diagnosis-dependent

That could help some families get practical support earlier. But the real test is whether that support exists in practice, not just in consultation documents.

### 3. Pathway quality will matter more

As commissioning becomes more structured, the difference between a rushed assessment and a well-run pathway may become more visible.

If you are currently waiting for an assessment, understanding what a good pathway looks like can help you navigate next steps more confidently.

## FAQs

### Are ADHD waiting lists getting worse in England?

Current data suggests demand has increased significantly, with long waiting times in many areas and large regional variation.

### Will SEND reform remove the need for diagnosis?

Not entirely. Diagnosis may become less central in some cases, depending on how reform is implemented locally.
