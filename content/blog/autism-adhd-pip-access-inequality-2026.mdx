Why have Personal Independence Payment (PIP) recipient rates linked to autism and ADHD risen fastest in some of England's least deprived areas? Does that mean need is now greater there, or that diagnosis automatically leads to financial support?

Neither conclusion follows from the data.

On **22 September 2026**, the Nuffield Trust published a new analysis of PIP receipt among 16- and 17-year-olds in England. It found a large rise across every level of deprivation between 2019 and 2025, but the fastest growth occurred in less deprived neighbourhoods. [Read the Nuffield Trust briefing](https://www.nuffieldtrust.org.uk/resource/pip-ADHD-and-autism-weakening-link-with-deprivation).

The analysis raises important questions about unequal access to diagnosis, recognition and the benefits system. It does not show that affluent young people are more likely to be autistic or have ADHD, and it cannot tell us why any individual claim was awarded.

## What did the new analysis find?

In June 2019, **16,045** 16- and 17-year-olds in England received PIP with autism or ADHD recorded as their main disabling condition. That was **1.3%** of the age group.

By June 2025, the number had risen to **51,148**, or **3.4%** of 16- and 17-year-olds.

The rise was not confined to one kind of area:

- in the least deprived fifth of neighbourhoods, the estimated recipient rate increased from **0.8% to 2.5%**;
- in the most deprived fifth, it increased from **2.1% to 5.1%**.

Rates therefore remained higher in the most deprived areas. The change is that the gap narrowed. In 2019, the rate in the most deprived fifth was about **2.5 times** the rate in the least deprived fifth. By 2025, it was about **twice** as high.

The Nuffield Trust's more detailed neighbourhood analysis found that the increase was around **28% faster** in the least deprived fifth than in the most deprived fifth. The authors tested alternative assumptions for missing small-area figures and reported that this difference remained statistically significant.

> PIP figures are not a head count of autistic or ADHD young people. They show who has been recognised as meeting a test based on day-to-day functional impact.

## What does PIP actually measure?

PIP is a non-means-tested benefit for people aged 16 and over who have a long-term physical or mental health condition or disability and experience difficulty with certain daily living or mobility activities. A person can receive it while working or having savings.

The award is based on how a person's condition affects them, not simply on the name of a condition. The assessment considers activities such as preparing food, managing treatment, communicating, making budgeting decisions, planning journeys and moving around.

This distinction matters for autism and ADHD. Two people with the same diagnosis can have very different support needs. Equally, a person may have substantial functional difficulties while still waiting for a formal assessment.

The Department for Work and Pensions' assessment guidance states that there is **no requirement to have a formal diagnosis**, although there must be enough evidence for the reported needs to be considered consistent. The Nuffield Trust updated its briefing on **25 September 2026** to correct wording that had implied diagnosis was a prerequisite.

That does not mean diagnosis is irrelevant. Clinical evidence can form part of a claim, and an assessment can help someone understand their needs. But diagnosis and PIP eligibility answer different questions: one is clinical; the other concerns the effect of a long-term condition on specified daily activities.

## Why might the pattern have changed?

The analysis identifies possibilities, not a single proven explanation.

### Unequal access to diagnosis and information

Previous research has found evidence of unequal access to autism and ADHD recognition. Families with more time, money or confidence navigating complex systems may be better placed to pursue private assessment, gather evidence and complete a benefits application.

That would not mean their needs are less real. It would mean that the route from experiencing difficulty to having that difficulty formally recognised may be easier for some groups than others.

### Greater awareness and take-up

Some young people may always have met the functional criteria but did not previously apply. Increased awareness of neurodivergence and of PIP could lead more eligible families to seek support.

The Nuffield Trust also notes that cost-of-living pressure may have encouraged households that previously managed without claiming to apply for help to which they were entitled. The analysis cannot measure this explanation directly.

### Ethnic inequalities

The researchers found that areas with a higher proportion of residents recorded as White had higher autism- and ADHD-related PIP recipient rates, even after deprivation was considered. The association was stronger in the most deprived areas.

This may point to barriers affecting minoritised ethnic groups, including differences in recognition, diagnosis, trust, information or access to PIP. It could also reflect cultural differences in how neurodivergent traits and disability are understood. Area-level data cannot determine which explanation applies to a particular family.

### Differences in how need is recognised

PIP requires people to translate everyday difficulties into a detailed administrative framework. Variation may arise not only from underlying need, but from who applies, what evidence is available and how consistently functional impact is understood and assessed.

> The responsible reading is not that less deprived families suddenly have greater need. It is that recognition, evidence and access to support may not be working evenly.

## What the figures do not prove

The briefing is careful about its limits, and those limits matter.

First, this is an **area-level analysis**. It compares local PIP rates with neighbourhood characteristics. It cannot assume that every recipient shares the average income, employment or ethnicity of the area in which they live.

Second, many small-area counts were suppressed to protect privacy, particularly in 2019 when recipient numbers were lower. The researchers estimated those missing values and tested different assumptions, but estimates remain estimates.

Third, the employment deprivation measure used in the analysis is based mainly on working-age benefit data from 2022 and 2023, rather than the circumstances of each 16- or 17-year-old.

Fourth, PIP records a **main disabling condition**. A young person may have more than one condition or support need, and the dataset cannot capture their full clinical or social picture.

The findings therefore do not establish overdiagnosis, inappropriate claims or a change in the underlying prevalence of autism and ADHD. They show a changing pattern in who receives support.

## What does this mean for families?

For a family considering PIP, the most useful lesson is that the application should describe **functional impact**, not rely on a diagnostic label alone.

The official claim form asks how a condition affects everyday life. Relevant supporting information can include care plans, prescription lists and evidence from professionals or other people involved in the young person's support. Clear examples of what happens, how often it happens, what help is needed and whether an activity can be completed safely and reliably are usually more informative than a list of diagnoses.

For children already receiving Disability Living Allowance (DLA), the transition at 16 also needs attention. The Department for Work and Pensions sends an invitation to apply for PIP. If the application is made by the deadline in the letter, DLA usually continues while the PIP claim is assessed. Missing the deadline can stop those payments.

The current [GOV.UK PIP guidance](https://www.gov.uk/pip) explains eligibility and the claim process. Families can also check the separate guidance on [what happens when a child receiving DLA turns 16](https://www.gov.uk/disability-living-allowance-children/when-your-child-turns-16). Scotland uses Adult Disability Payment instead of PIP, and Northern Ireland has a separate administration. The Nuffield Trust analysis covers England only.

## What should services take from this?

Benefits data should not be treated as a shortcut for estimating how many young people are autistic or have ADHD. It reflects clinical recognition, functional impairment, awareness, evidence and access to an application process.

The findings also strengthen the case for support that does not depend unnecessarily on a diagnostic label. Schools, health services and wider public systems can respond to identified needs while an assessment is pending, rather than making diagnosis the only gateway to help.

At the same time, fair access to high-quality assessment still matters. A system in which some families can navigate assessment and benefits more easily than others risks reproducing inequality, even when the rules appear the same on paper.

The new figures are most useful when read as a warning against simple explanations. PIP receipt has increased sharply, but the pattern cannot be reduced to prevalence, diagnosis or deprivation alone. The practical question is whether young people with comparable needs have a comparable chance of being recognised and supported.
