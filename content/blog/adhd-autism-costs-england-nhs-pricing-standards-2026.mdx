## Quick summary
- On 28 August, the NHS Alliance warned that ADHD and autism spending had become unsustainable in some local systems, citing very large increases reported by individual ICBs.
- These examples are not national spending totals. They are local cases used to illustrate a commissioning problem.
- The concern is not simply that more people are being assessed. It is that demand has grown alongside a rapidly expanding provider market, large price variation and fragmented contracts.
- NHS England has already introduced national guide prices for 2026/27, but they are non-mandatory benchmarks rather than a fixed national tariff.
- The next policy debate is therefore likely to focus on both price and service specification: what an NHS-funded assessment should cost, what it should contain and what happens after it.

At the end of August, a stark phrase entered the ADHD and autism debate: costs were “running out of control”.
The wording came from the NHS Alliance, which represents organisations across the health and care system. It said some integrated care boards were seeing spending on ADHD and autism services rise extremely quickly and called for a mandatory national tariff and a common service specification.
The headline is easy to read as a story about “too many assessments”.
That would be a mistake.
The more useful question is why similar NHS-funded pathways can be bought in so many different ways, from so many providers, at such different prices — and why commissioners are now asking for more national consistency.
That matters to patients too. A payment system is not just an accounting exercise. Increasingly, it is being used to define what a complete, credible assessment pathway should include.

## 1) What NHS leaders actually warned about
The NHS Alliance says spending in some areas has risen by as much as tenfold.
Among the examples it reported, one ICB said its monthly spending had increased from £146,000 to £1.5 million. Another reported adult ADHD spending rising from £11 million in 2023/24 to a projected £51 million in the current financial year.
Those are dramatic figures, but an important caveat is needed: the ICBs were not named in the public statement and the examples should not be treated as a national estimate of ADHD or autism spending.
They tell us something different.
Local commissioners are reporting that a service area which was once relatively contained has become much harder to predict financially. The NHS Alliance links this to rising demand, insufficient local NHS capacity and a rapid increase in the number of independent providers delivering NHS-funded activity.
One ICB reportedly now deals with around 80 providers. Another said the number of provider invoices it receives had risen from roughly 12–14 to 48.
That creates an administrative problem as well as a financial one. Every additional contractual relationship can involve different prices, different service boundaries, different reporting and different arrangements for what happens after diagnosis.
### Nova take
The headline spend is striking. The more important signal is the variation and fragmentation underneath it.
## 2) This is not simply a story about “too many diagnoses”
Demand for ADHD and autism assessment has increased substantially. That is already well established.
But cost growth can come from several sources at the same time:
- more referrals and assessments
- longer or more complex pathways
- greater use of independent sector capacity
- different prices for similar activity
- duplicated activity or unnecessary reassessment
- disconnected assessment and treatment pathways
- administrative overhead created by many provider relationships
- treatment and follow-up costs that appear after diagnosis
Reducing the whole issue to diagnosis rates would therefore blur together demand, capacity, commissioning and quality.
It would also miss an uncomfortable point: expanding independent capacity has helped some people access assessment sooner. The NHS Alliance itself acknowledges that additional providers have helped ease some delays.
The policy challenge is not simply whether independent capacity should exist. It is how an NHS-funded system can buy that capacity consistently, know what it is paying for, and connect it properly to ongoing care.
This is why the current debate is moving away from a simple NHS-versus-private argument.
### Nova take
More capacity can reduce delay and still create new commissioning problems. Both things can be true at once.
## 3) Price variation is now impossible to ignore
The NHS Alliance says one ICB reported paying between £300 and £3,000 for similar services.
Again, that is a local example rather than a national price survey. But the range helps explain why commissioners are asking for stronger national rules.
An assessment price cannot be interpreted properly without knowing what is included.
A low headline price might cover a narrow diagnostic episode. A higher price might include more professional time, developmental history, informant evidence, multidisciplinary discussion, feedback, reporting or elements of follow-up. Conversely, a high price does not guarantee that all of those things are present.
That is why a tariff on its own would not solve the problem.
If two providers are paid the same amount but one offers only assessment while another carries responsibility for a broader pathway, the apparent standardisation is misleading.
The more useful question is: what activity sits behind the price?
NHS England has started answering that through the 2026/27 Payment Scheme.
### Nova take
A cheaper assessment is not automatically better value, and a higher price does not automatically mean a stronger pathway.
## 4) NHS England already has national guide prices — but they are not mandatory
The 2026/27 NHS Payment Scheme introduced guide prices for ADHD and autism assessment services.
After consultation, NHS England increased several of the proposed figures. The final published assessment guide prices include:
- **Adult ADHD, face-to-face:** £850
- **Adult ADHD, virtual:** £700
- **Child and young person ADHD, face-to-face:** £950
- **Adult autism, face-to-face:** £1,150
- **Child and young person autism, face-to-face:** £1,350
- **Adult combined ADHD and autism, face-to-face:** £1,300
- **Child and young person combined assessment, face-to-face:** £1,500
The important word is *guide*.
NHS England explicitly describes these prices as non-mandatory benchmarks. Commissioners and providers can agree different local prices where the delivery model or patient complexity justifies it.
That makes the NHS Alliance’s August call more specific than it first appears. It is not asking for the creation of national pricing from nothing. A national reference point already exists.
It is asking to go further — towards a mandatory tariff and a national service specification.
That is a much larger policy step.
### Nova take
England now has a benchmark. The unresolved question is whether a benchmark is strong enough to control a fragmented market.
## 5) Payment guidance is beginning to define the pathway, not just the bill
The most interesting part of NHS England’s work is arguably not the pound figures.
Its payment guidance sets expectations around how services should be commissioned.
Among other things, it says:
- shared-care arrangements should support end-to-end pathways
- providers that cannot deliver every part of a pathway should have clear handover processes
- pre-diagnostic activity and environmental adjustments should be properly resourced
- unnecessary reassessments should be avoided
- independent diagnostic reports should be recognised by NHS-commissioned titration services unless there is a clear clinical reason not to
- providers should meet national data and reporting obligations
- ADHD diagnosis should involve a full clinical and psychosocial assessment by appropriately qualified professionals
- autism diagnostic services should have appropriate multidisciplinary expertise
In other words, the payment framework is also becoming a quality framework.
This is important because the true cost of a fragmented pathway can appear later. If an assessment cannot be used by the next service, if a patient needs to be assessed again, or if nobody is responsible for medication titration, the system may pay twice while the patient waits longer.
A price attached to an incomplete episode can therefore look cheap while producing more downstream cost.
### Nova take
The cheapest assessment on an invoice may become expensive if the pathway has to be rebuilt afterwards.
## 6) Why a national service specification may matter more than a national tariff
NHS England says there is currently wide variation in local care models and no nationally mandated service specification from which all ADHD and autism care is developed.
That wording needs nuance. Autism services already have national guidance, including the 2023 national framework and operational guidance. NICE also provides clinical guidance for both autism and ADHD.
What is missing is a single mandatory commissioning specification that tells every NHS-funded provider exactly what must be delivered under a defined service.
That is what the NHS Alliance is now asking for alongside a tariff.
A stronger service specification could make it easier to compare providers on meaningful terms:
- which professionals are involved
- what information must be gathered
- how school or informant evidence is used
- how differential diagnoses and co-occurring conditions are considered
- what constitutes completion of assessment
- what the report must contain
- who provides feedback
- how handover works
- what is included for titration or follow-up
- what data providers must submit
It would not remove clinical judgement or make every assessment identical. Nor should it.
It could, however, make the commissioning unit clearer: not simply “one diagnosis”, but a defined episode of clinically robust care.
### Nova take
The next phase of reform is likely to be about defining what providers deliver, not only what they charge.
## 7) What families and adults should take from the cost debate
Patients should not be expected to become NHS finance experts.
But the funding debate does explain why some pathways are becoming more structured and why questions about provider scope matter.
When comparing or entering an assessment pathway, useful questions include:
- What exactly is included in the assessment?
- Which professionals are involved?
- Is developmental history gathered?
- Is information from school, family or an informant included where relevant?
- Will there be a feedback appointment and detailed report?
- If ADHD medication is appropriate, does the provider offer titration?
- If not, who receives the handover?
- Will the provider submit the required NHS data?
- What happens if another NHS service needs clarification about the report?
These are quality questions, but they are also commissioning questions.

## What this means in practice
The August warning should not be interpreted as evidence that ADHD or autism assessment itself is an inappropriate use of NHS money.
It is better understood as evidence that a rapidly expanding area of healthcare has outgrown the loose commissioning arrangements around it.
Three policy directions now appear increasingly likely:
- **greater price consistency**, whether through stronger guide prices or eventually a tariff
- **clearer national service expectations**, so commissioners know what they are buying
- **more emphasis on end-to-end pathways**, rather than paying for isolated assessment activity that does not connect to treatment or support
For patients, that could ultimately be positive if standardisation improves clarity and quality.
The risk is that financial control becomes the dominant objective and access is tightened without building enough local capacity.
The test for any reform is therefore not simply whether spending becomes more predictable. It is whether patients receive a pathway that is timely, clinically robust and usable afterwards.
## FAQs
### Are ADHD and autism costs really rising tenfold across England?
No national tenfold figure has been published. The NHS Alliance said spending had risen by up to tenfold in some areas and cited examples from individual ICBs. Those examples should not be treated as national totals.
### Is there already an NHS price for ADHD and autism assessment?
NHS England introduced national guide prices for 2026/27. They are benchmarks, not mandatory tariffs, and local prices can differ.
### Why are the guide prices different for adults and children?
Pathway design, professional input and the information required can differ between age groups and assessment types. The guide prices are intended to represent average activity rather than a universal cost for every individual case.
### Does standard pricing guarantee a good assessment?
No. A price can help commissioners compare activity, but clinical quality depends on what the pathway includes, who delivers it, how evidence is gathered and what happens afterwards.
### Could a mandatory tariff reduce access?
It could have different effects depending on how it is designed. A realistic tariff and service specification could improve consistency; a price set below the cost of delivering a robust pathway could make provision harder. That is why price and quality standards need to be considered together.
