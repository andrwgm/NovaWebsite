## Quick summary

- A new UK government report published on 11 September 2026 found that most disabled employees who ask for reasonable adjustments receive at least some support, but delays, unclear processes and knowledge gaps still create problems.
- For autistic and ADHD employees, the practical issue is often not knowing exactly what to ask for, who owns the request, or how long the process should take.
- A formal diagnosis is not required for someone to be considered disabled under the Equality Act 2010. Acas says employers should support neurodivergent workers whether or not they have a diagnosis.
- If you need an adjustment, make the barrier and the change you are asking for as concrete as possible, keep a written record, and agree how and when the request will be reviewed.

## What has changed this week?

On 11 September 2026, the UK government published new research into delays in workplace reasonable-adjustment decisions. The report was commissioned by the Disability Unit and looks at evidence from employees, employers, academic research, Acas conciliators and Employment Tribunal cases.

The central finding is more nuanced than “employers are refusing adjustments”. The report says most disabled employees who request adjustments receive all or some of what they ask for. The bigger problems are often uncertainty, poor processes and delays.

The report identifies five recurring barriers: delays, knowledge gaps, fear of negative consequences, unclear workplace processes and cost.

That matters for neurodivergent workers because the report also notes growing numbers of cases involving mental health and neurodiversity, and says these requests can involve procedural changes such as flexible working. Those can be harder for managers to understand and implement than a straightforward piece of equipment.

For autistic and ADHD employees, this is useful because it shifts the conversation from “Do I deserve support?” to a more practical question: “What is making the job harder, and what change could reduce that disadvantage?”

## What the report says about delays

The evidence does not show that every reasonable-adjustment request is routinely delayed. In fact, the government report is careful about the limits of the available data.

But some employees do wait a long time.

The University of York research cited in the report found that 23% of disabled workers surveyed waited more than a month for a decision on an adjustment request, while 9.8% waited more than three months.

The report also found that delays can happen after a decision has effectively been made. Employers may be waiting for occupational health advice, specialist equipment, external funding or another service to put the adjustment in place.

The important point is that a delay can still matter even when the employer intends to help. If someone is struggling with sensory overload, unpredictable working patterns, communication demands or executive-function pressures, waiting weeks or months can affect performance, attendance and wellbeing.

The report also says that intentional delay appears to be less common than confusion, lack of knowledge or administrative bottlenecks. That distinction matters because it changes what may help: clearer ownership, clearer communication and a written next step can sometimes be more useful than assuming the request has simply been rejected.

## Do you need an autism or ADHD diagnosis to ask for adjustments?

Not necessarily.

Acas states that a worker does not need a diagnosis to be considered disabled under the Equality Act 2010. It also says employers should offer support for neurodivergence whether or not someone has a formal diagnosis.

The legal duty depends on whether a person meets the Equality Act definition of disability and on the circumstances of the disadvantage they face. Being autistic or having ADHD does not make every requested change automatically reasonable, and an employer does not have to agree to every proposal.

But lack of a formal diagnosis should not be treated as a reason to avoid the conversation altogether.

This is especially relevant while NHS assessment waits remain long. Someone may know that particular parts of work are difficult long before they have completed a diagnostic process.

## Start with the barrier, not the label

One of the most useful ways to prepare a reasonable-adjustment request is to describe what happens at work in concrete terms.

Instead of starting with:

> “I have ADHD, so I need more flexibility.”

You might explain:

> “Frequent last-minute changes to priorities mean I lose track of what is urgent and miss deadlines. A written priority list after our weekly planning meeting would help me organise the work accurately.”

Or instead of:

> “I am autistic and the office is overwhelming.”

You might explain:

> “On busy office days, background conversations make it difficult for me to process written work. I would like to discuss using noise-cancelling headphones for focused tasks and having access to a quieter desk when one is available.”

Those examples are not universal solutions. Different people with the same diagnosis can need completely different adjustments.

The aim is to make three things clear:

- the barrier you are experiencing;
- how it affects your work;
- the change you think could reduce that disadvantage.

That gives the employer something specific to discuss.

## Examples of adjustments that may help neurodivergent workers

Acas gives examples of neurodiversity-related support and stresses that adjustments should be individual.

Depending on the job and the person, useful changes might include:

- written follow-up after verbal instructions;
- clearer priorities and deadlines;
- advance notice of changes where possible;
- a quieter workspace or permission to use noise-cancelling headphones;
- changes to meeting format or communication style;
- extra processing time for complex information;
- regular check-ins with a named manager;
- changes to working patterns;
- assistive software or other equipment.

An adjustment still has to be reasonable in the context of the role and organisation. Cost, practicality, effectiveness and health and safety can all be relevant.

If one proposal is not workable, Acas says the employer should still discuss the request and look for another reasonable way to provide support.

## Put the request in writing

You can raise an adjustment in a conversation, but keeping a written record can make the process much easier to follow.

Acas recommends confirming agreed adjustments in writing and advises workers asking for adjustments to consider making the request in writing as well as discussing it.

A simple email can include:

- what is happening;
- the disadvantage it creates;
- the adjustment you are asking to try;
- whether you are open to alternatives;
- when you would like to review whether it is working.

You do not need to produce a legal document.

For example:

> “I am finding it difficult to follow verbal changes to priorities when several tasks change at once. Could we trial a short written summary after our Monday planning meeting for four weeks, then review whether it is helping?”

A trial period can be useful when nobody is sure whether a change will work in practice.

## What if your employer has not replied?

The new government report does not set a universal deadline for every request, and the law does not provide one simple number of days that applies to all adjustments.

That does not mean an employer can leave a request unanswered indefinitely.

If you are waiting, a practical follow-up can ask:

- who is responsible for the decision;
- whether more information is needed from you;
- what the next step is;
- when you can expect an update;
- whether any temporary support can be used while the full request is considered.

The report found that employers and employees can have very different expectations about communication. In the University of York research it cites, employees thought updates roughly every 4.3 days were reasonable, while employers thought around 8.4 days was reasonable.

You do not need to use those figures as a rule. They simply illustrate why agreeing a communication plan can prevent silence from becoming another source of stress.

If an employer refuses an adjustment because it is not considered reasonable, Acas says they should explain the decision and try to find another way to support the person.

## Reasonable adjustments and Access to Work are not the same thing

This distinction causes confusion.

Reasonable adjustments are changes an employer may have a legal duty to make. Access to Work is a government scheme that can fund additional practical support related to disability or a health condition.

Government guidance is explicit that Access to Work does not replace an employer's duty to make reasonable adjustments.

Depending on eligibility and need, Access to Work may help with things such as specialist equipment, adaptations, support workers, travel or other practical assistance. It can also complement adjustments that the employer is already making.

If you are unsure which route applies, it can be worth checking both the Access to Work guidance and your employer's own process.

## If the process is becoming difficult

If you feel stuck, try to keep the issue specific.

Write down:

1. what you asked for;
2. when you asked;
3. what response you received;
4. what is still unresolved;
5. what you want to happen next.

You may be able to resolve the issue informally with your manager or HR team. If not, Acas provides guidance on reasonable adjustments and disability discrimination, including what to do if an employer does not make an adjustment.

You can also contact the Acas helpline for workplace advice.

If the situation has become a formal dispute, get individual advice rather than relying on a general blog article. Employment law depends on the facts of each case.

## What this means for autistic and ADHD adults

The most useful message from the new government research is not that the reasonable-adjustment system is broken or that every request will be straightforward.

It is that uncertainty itself is a major part of the problem.

For neurodivergent employees, clearer requests can help: identify the barrier, suggest a change, ask who owns the decision and agree when it will be reviewed.

For employers, the same report points towards clearer processes, better manager understanding and more consistent communication.

And if you are still waiting for an autism or ADHD assessment, you do not necessarily need to wait for a diagnostic report before discussing support at work.

The practical goal is not to prove a label. It is to identify what would make it possible to do the job more effectively and sustainably.
