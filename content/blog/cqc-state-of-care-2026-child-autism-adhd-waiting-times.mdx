Published on 23 October 2026, the Care Quality Commission's latest State of Care report presents a troubling contrast: some NHS waiting times for adults have improved, while long waits for children's community health services have grown sharply.

For families seeking autism or ADHD assessments, the findings are relevant — but the numbers need careful reading. The report does **not** say that 80,000 children are waiting specifically for an autism or ADHD diagnosis. It says more than 80,000 children and young people in England were waiting over a year for a community health appointment, with the majority of those very long waits in community paediatrics, which includes neurodevelopmental assessment.

The practical question is what families can do when a child's needs are affecting daily life but an assessment remains months, or even years, away.

## What the CQC found

The [CQC's State of Care 2025/26 report](https://www.cqc.org.uk/publications/major-report/state-care/2025-2026/summary), published on **23 October 2026**, reports that:

- **More than 80,000 children and young people** in England had waited over a year for a community health appointment — almost 11 times the corresponding number of adults.
- The number of children and young people waiting more than a year for community health services **rose by 129% in the two years to March 2026**, while the number of adults waiting that long fell.
- The majority of children's waits exceeding a year were for **community paediatric services**, which include assessment of neurodevelopmental conditions such as autism and ADHD.
- The average monthly number of children waiting for first contact with mental health services increased **31% between 2023/24 and 2025/26**, from 237,590 to 311,070.

These figures describe different services and measures. The mental health waiting-list figures should not be added to the community health figure, and neither can be treated as an autism or ADHD diagnostic waiting-list total.

The CQC's finding is nevertheless substantial: long waits for children are moving in the wrong direction, and neurodevelopmental pathways form an important part of the pressure.

## Why the distinction between community paediatrics and diagnostic waiting lists matters

Community health services are broader than diagnostic clinics. They include community paediatrics, speech and language therapy, school nursing and other care delivered outside hospital settings.

Community paediatric teams may assess children with suspected autism or ADHD, but they also see children with other developmental, physical and complex health needs. Some children receive neurodevelopmental assessments through separate NHS pathways.

That means **the 80,000 figure is not an estimate of children awaiting an autism or ADHD diagnosis**. Presenting it that way would overstate what the regulator has measured.

It also means an individual family's waiting time cannot be calculated from this national figure. Waiting lists differ by local service, referral route, age group and clinical need.

What the report does show is that services supporting children with developmental and health needs are struggling to keep pace with demand. This is a wider problem than any one diagnostic label.

## What is new — and what has not changed?

This is a new national assessment of service performance, not an announcement of a new referral rule.

**What is new:** the CQC has published its 2025/26 findings, including the 129% increase in year-long waits over two years and the continuing imbalance between children and adults.

**What has not changed because of this report:** it does not cancel referrals, introduce a national autism or ADHD assessment deadline, change Right to Choose eligibility, or automatically provide additional appointments.

The findings also do not mean that every child must wait over a year. Some will be assessed sooner; others will wait longer. The relevant service can explain the route and what information it can provide about a child's referral.

The timing matters because the report follows the government's **9 October 2026 response to the Fonagy review**, which emphasised earlier support based on need rather than waiting for a diagnosis. That response set out a policy direction. The CQC report provides further evidence of why delivering support during the wait matters, but it does not establish that new local support arrangements are already available everywhere.

## If your child is waiting for an autism or ADHD assessment

A waiting-list letter is important, but it should not be the only plan for the months ahead.

### 1. Check that the referral has reached the right service

Ask the referring GP, school or other professional which service received the referral, whether it has been accepted and who should be contacted about changes in circumstances.

If a service asks for questionnaires, school observations or developmental information, keep copies and check whether anything remains outstanding. Do not assume that completing forms guarantees a particular appointment date.

For an autism assessment, the [NHS advises](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/autism/assessments/) speaking to a GP or education professional about support available while waiting.

### 2. Speak to the SENCO about what is difficult now

For children in school, support should be discussed in terms of the difficulties they are experiencing, not postponed until a diagnostic report arrives.

For example, a child might be struggling with transitions, sensory demands, following multi-step instructions, concentration, attendance or communication. The school can consider what changes would help, monitor their effect and review them with the family.

The government's [SEN support guidance](https://www.gov.uk/children-with-special-educational-needs/special-educational-needs-support) explains the support schools and colleges can provide. The [NHS guidance on ADHD in children](https://www.nhs.uk/conditions/adhd-children-teenagers/) likewise says children should continue to receive support at home and school while waiting for referral or assessment.

It may help to ask for a short written record of the difficulties identified, the support being tried, who is responsible and when it will be reviewed. That record can also be useful when the assessment eventually takes place.

### 3. Keep an accurate picture of your child's needs

A brief record is usually more helpful than trying to document every difficult moment.

Note patterns that affect daily life: when difficulties arise, what seems to make them better or worse, how they affect learning or wellbeing, and what support has been tried. Include your child's strengths and their own account where possible.

Share significant changes with the professionals involved. If a child becomes more distressed, stops attending school, experiences a marked deterioration or develops new health concerns, seek appropriate support rather than waiting for the assessment date. If there is an immediate safety concern, use urgent or emergency NHS services.

### 4. Understand the difference between diagnosis and an EHC needs assessment

An autism or ADHD diagnostic assessment is a clinical process. An **Education, Health and Care (EHC) needs assessment** is a separate process used to decide whether a child or young person may need an Education, Health and Care Plan (EHCP).

Parents can ask their local authority for an EHC needs assessment if they believe their child may need support beyond what is ordinarily available through SEN support. They do not have to wait for an autism or ADHD diagnosis before making that request.

The government's [EHCP guidance](https://www.gov.uk/children-with-special-educational-needs/extra-SEN-help) explains how requests and decisions work in England.

Not every child waiting for a clinical assessment needs an EHCP. The decision should be based on their educational needs and the provision required, not simply the length of a diagnostic waiting list.

### 5. Check alternatives without giving up an existing place prematurely

Some families consider Right to Choose or a private assessment because of long NHS waits. These are different routes, with different eligibility, funding and follow-up arrangements.

Right to Choose applies to eligible referrals in **England** and does not guarantee a particular advertised waiting time. Local commissioning arrangements can affect the speed of an NHS-funded appointment.

A private assessment may be an option for some families, but it involves costs and does not automatically guarantee NHS medication prescribing or shared care if ADHD is diagnosed. Ask what is included in the full pathway, including feedback, reports and any treatment or follow-up that may be needed.

Before cancelling or changing an existing NHS referral, check with the relevant provider or commissioner what will happen to time already spent waiting.

## What the CQC report means for schools and services

The CQC is reporting a system-wide problem, not blaming individual families for seeking help or clinicians for the growth in referrals.

One useful reading of its findings is that a child's support cannot depend entirely on the speed of a diagnostic pathway. Schools, primary care, community teams and specialist services have different responsibilities, and families often need them to work together while an assessment is pending.

The report also raises a broader question for commissioners: whether progress in reducing some adult waiting times is being matched by improvements in children's community services. The CQC's answer, on the evidence available for 2025/26, is no.

That is a finding about the system. It is **not** proof that a particular child's difficulties have been caused by the waiting list, or that a particular intervention would have prevented them. Individual needs still require individual assessment.

## What families should take from the findings

The report gives national weight to a problem many families already recognise: children can spend a significant part of their school years waiting for access to community health services.

It does not offer an immediate fix, and it would be misleading to suggest that a new report alone shortens a queue.

The practical response is to work on two tracks at once: keep the assessment pathway moving, and seek appropriate support for the child's needs in the meantime. Ask clear questions, keep a record of what has been tried and seek further help when circumstances change.

## Frequently asked questions

### Are 80,000 children waiting for an autism or ADHD diagnosis?

No. The CQC figure covers children and young people waiting over a year for **community health services** in England. Many of the longest waits are in community paediatrics, which includes autism and ADHD assessment, but the figure is not a diagnosis-specific count.

### Does the CQC report mean my child will wait longer?

Not necessarily. It describes national trends using data up to March 2026. Your child's waiting time depends on the local pathway and their individual referral. Ask the service holding the referral for information specific to your situation.

### Can school provide SEN support before an autism or ADHD diagnosis?

Yes. In England, schools should identify and respond to special educational needs based on the child's needs. A diagnostic label is not a general prerequisite for SEN support.

### Can I request an EHCP assessment while waiting for a diagnosis?

You can request an EHC needs assessment from your local authority if you believe your child may need an EHCP. It is a separate decision from a clinical autism or ADHD assessment.

### Has Right to Choose changed because of this report?

No. The CQC's State of Care report does not change Right to Choose law or local referral arrangements. Check the current NHS-funded pathway and waiting arrangements before making decisions about changing provider.
