On 9 October 2026, the government published a major independent review of mental health conditions, ADHD and autism in England. Its central argument is that people should receive practical help when they need it, rather than having to wait for a diagnostic label before anything can happen.

The review also calls for tighter oversight of ADHD and autism assessments, changes to how NHS services are commissioned, and more consistent support after diagnosis. The government has responded with commitments to pursue regulation, review advertising and consult on national NHS-funded prices.

**These are significant policy developments, but they are not all changes that have taken effect today.** If you are waiting for an assessment, considering a private provider or supporting a child at school, the distinction between a recommendation, a government commitment and an implemented rule matters.

## At a glance: what is confirmed?

- **The review is final.** Led by Professor Peter Fonagy, it was published by the Department of Health and Social Care on 9 October 2026.
- **Diagnosis is not being abolished.** The review explicitly says that appropriate diagnosis remains essential, including where clinical treatment is needed.
- **The government wants earlier, needs-led support.** Its forthcoming mental health strategy will set out how this is to be delivered.
- **Broader CQC oversight is planned, not yet universally in force.** The government says it will work towards making all ADHD and autism diagnostic assessments a CQC-regulated activity, including privately funded assessments.
- **Advertising has not been banned.** The review recommends banning direct-to-public advertising of NHS-funded ADHD and autism assessments; the government has instead announced a review of advertising practices.
- **A national NHS tariff is proposed, not finalised.** NHS England will consult on prices for NHS-funded services and intends to introduce new payment arrangements from April 2027.
- **Existing referrals have not been cancelled by this announcement.** It does not itself change an individual's appointment, Right to Choose entitlement or local waiting-list position.

## What is the Fonagy review, and why does it matter?

The independent review examined the rise in demand for mental health and neurodevelopmental support, including ADHD and autism assessments, and how the current system responds. It considered clinical evidence, administrative data, population surveys and the experiences of people using and delivering services.

Its most important distinction is between **how many people have a condition**, **how many have a recorded diagnosis** and **how many are seeking help**. Those figures measure different things.

For example, the review reports more than **560,000 open ADHD referrals at the end of 2025** and more than **254,000 open autism referrals by December 2025**. These are historical figures used in the report, not a live count for October 2026, and the categories should not simply be added together as a definitive count of unique people.

The review finds that recorded diagnoses and demand for assessment have increased sharply, while up-to-date population prevalence evidence is limited. It does **not** conclude that ADHD or autism is being overdiagnosed on a large scale. It does warn that underdiagnosis, misdiagnosis and overdiagnosis all need attention as services come under pressure.

That nuance is important. A person who has waited years for an explanation of their difficulties should not be told that increased recognition makes their needs less real. Equally, a good assessment must be capable of identifying a different or additional explanation when the evidence points that way.

## 1. Earlier support without waiting for a diagnosis

The review recommends a move from diagnosis-dependent access towards what it calls **“needs-led plus diagnosis”**. The idea is not to replace clinical assessment with generic advice. It is to offer appropriate support earlier while keeping access to diagnosis and specialist treatment when required.

The proposed model would look at the person's difficulties, their impact on daily life and any risks, rather than relying on one label to determine whether help begins.

For a child, that might mean school-based adjustments, communication support or input from relevant professionals while an autism or ADHD assessment is pending. For an adult, it might mean practical workplace adjustments or help with difficulties affecting everyday functioning, alongside a referral where clinically appropriate.

This is a direction for future service design, **not a promise that every NHS area has a new support pathway available today**. Local services, eligibility and capacity still vary.

The review also proposes clinically led reviews of existing ADHD and autism waiting lists. Importantly, it says people should **not** simply be removed from a list without appropriate help being arranged. That is a recommendation about how future pathways should operate, not an instruction that current referrals are now being reassessed or cancelled.

### Does this make diagnosis less important?

No. A diagnostic assessment can clarify whether ADHD, autism, another condition or several overlapping factors best explain someone's experiences. For ADHD, appropriate assessment is also part of deciding whether medication and ongoing clinical monitoring may be suitable.

The point is that a person may need useful support *before* that clinical question is resolved. Support based on need and accurate diagnosis are complementary, not competing goals.

## 2. The government plans wider regulation of ADHD and autism assessments

This is one of the most consequential announcements for anyone comparing assessment providers.

The review recommends making all ADHD and autism diagnostic assessments subject to Care Quality Commission (CQC) regulation. The government says it will work with NHS England and CQC towards that outcome, covering NHS-funded and self-funded patients.

**This does not mean that every assessment provider has become newly CQC-regulated overnight.** The government has announced an intended regulatory change. The scope, legal mechanism, implementation timetable and detailed requirements still need to be established.

The direction is nevertheless significant: the government wants more consistent oversight of assessment quality, regardless of how an appointment is funded.

### What makes an assessment robust?

A credible assessment should gather enough evidence to understand the person's developmental and clinical history, current experiences, functional impact and possible co-occurring or alternative explanations. The relevant clinical guidance and the person's circumstances should shape the process.

The review raises concerns about assessments that are too brief, fragmented or narrowly focused on one diagnosis. That should not be simplified into a claim that every online assessment is unreliable. **The quality of an assessment depends on the clinical process and evidence gathered, not simply whether an appointment takes place through a screen.**

If you are choosing a provider, ask who conducts the assessment, how information from different settings is considered, what happens when the picture is unclear, whether a multidisciplinary review is appropriate and what report and follow-up you will receive.

Nova's [child autism assessment guide](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/child-autism-assessment-what-to-expect) explains the stages of gathering information, clinical review and feedback. These are questions worth asking of any provider, including Nova.

## 3. Has the government banned advertising for private ADHD or autism assessments?

No.

The review recommends a ban on **direct-to-public advertising of NHS-funded ADHD and autism assessments**. The government's published response is narrower: the Health Secretary will ask the Advertising Standards Authority to review advertising practices relating to autism and ADHD services.

That is an important difference. A recommendation in an independent report is not an advertising law, and a request for regulatory scrutiny is not itself a prohibition.

The government has not announced a general ban on clinics explaining their services or on people seeking a private assessment. Providers should, however, expect closer attention to the accuracy and balance of their claims.

For patients, the practical advice is to look beyond promises about speed. Check what is included in the price, what clinical evidence is gathered, who is responsible for the outcome, and what support follows.

## 4. What about new NHS prices and Right to Choose?

The government says **NHS England will consult on a national set of prices for NHS-funded ADHD and autism services**, including assessment, post-diagnostic support and follow-up. Its stated intention is to introduce final payment arrangements from **April 2027**.

This is not a new price list that applies today. Nor is it a cap on what a patient can be charged for a self-funded private assessment. The announcement concerns the proposed payment arrangements for NHS-funded services, and the consultation has yet to determine the final detail.

The distinction matters for Right to Choose. Right to Choose is an NHS-funded route where eligibility conditions are met; a private self-funded assessment is a different arrangement. The new review does not abolish the NHS Choice Framework or instantly change the terms of an existing referral.

However, commissioning and payment decisions can affect how quickly NHS-funded appointments are delivered. Nova has already explained this in its article on [78-week minimum waits in some Right to Choose pathways](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/right-to-choose-78-week-minimum-wait-adhd-autism-2026).

The new review adds a national policy direction to those existing local pressures. It does **not** resolve current waiting times or replace the need to check the arrangements that apply to your ICB and provider.

## 5. Is NICE changing its ADHD and autism guidance?

The government says it will work with NICE to consider the implications of the review for its existing guidance. That is a commitment to examine the guidance, not an announcement that new diagnostic criteria or assessment standards are already in force.

Current published guidance remains the reference point while any review proceeds. The report also recommends more integrated assessments that consider mental health difficulties alongside possible neurodevelopmental conditions.

## 6. What does the report mean for schools and families?

The review argues that children should not have to wait for a diagnosis before their difficulties are taken seriously. It supports earlier help through schools and communities and better links between education, health and specialist services.

The government connects this approach to its existing SEND reforms, school training and Experts at Hand programme. Those programmes were announced separately; **they are not newly created by the Fonagy review**.

For a family waiting for an assessment, the practical next step is to describe the child's needs clearly to their school or SENCO: what happens during the school day, what helps, what makes participation harder and what support can be tried and reviewed.

A diagnosis can be valuable, but support for identified special educational needs should not automatically be postponed until a diagnosis is available. For more detail, see our guide to [DfE-funded neurodiversity training for schools](https://www.novaclinics.co.uk/blog/neurodiversity-training-schools-dfe-2026).

## 7. What should you do if you are already waiting for an assessment?

There is no reason to cancel an existing referral because this report has been published. Instead, confirm that the provider has accepted your referral and ask which waiting-time information applies to your particular NHS or private pathway.

For children, raise specific barriers with the school and relevant health professionals. Adults can discuss the impact on daily life with their GP or another appropriate professional and consider practical adjustments at work.

Ask what evidence is collected during assessment, whether other explanations will be considered, what the report includes and what happens afterwards.

## What has actually changed — and what happens next?

The clearest immediate change is **policy direction**. The government has formally responded to a major national review and announced work on wider regulation, advertising oversight, national NHS payment arrangements and its next mental health strategy.

The changes that matter most to patients will depend on what follows: the content of consultations, any new legal or regulatory requirements, revised clinical guidance, and whether earlier support becomes genuinely available in local services.

It would be wrong to say that diagnosis is being removed as a route to care. It would also be wrong to suggest that a promise of needs-led support has already shortened today's waiting lists.

The more useful reading is that the system is being asked to do two things better at once: **offer help before a diagnosis where appropriate, and make diagnosis and follow-up more reliable when they are needed**.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does the review mean I can no longer get an ADHD or autism diagnosis?

No. The review supports timely, appropriate diagnosis. It argues that support should also be available according to need before diagnosis where possible.

### Are private assessments being banned?

No. The government plans to strengthen regulation of assessments, including self-funded assessments. It has not announced a ban on private assessment.

### Does the announcement make remote assessments invalid?

No. The review calls for stronger quality standards and better integrated assessment processes. It does not say that all remote assessments are invalid.

### Has Right to Choose changed today?

The review and government announcement do not themselves remove Right to Choose. Local commissioning arrangements and waiting times remain important and should be checked separately.

### Is there a new national maximum price for an ADHD assessment?

Not yet. NHS England is expected to consult on national payment arrangements for **NHS-funded** ADHD and autism services, with implementation intended from April 2027. That is different from setting a price for self-funded private assessments.

### Do schools now have to provide a new package of support?

The report does not create an immediate new legal entitlement. Existing duties and local SEND arrangements continue while the government develops its broader reforms.
