## Quick summary

- NHS North East and North Cumbria says eligible Right to Choose referrals for ADHD and autism remain open.
- For 2026/27, providers funded by the ICB are working to an approximately **78-week minimum wait for an initial assessment**.
- This is **not a new national 78-week rule** for everyone using Right to Choose in England. It is a local commissioning arrangement in North East and North Cumbria.
- A provider may advertise a much shorter waiting time than the wait that applies to an NHS-funded referral from a particular ICB.
- The practical question is therefore no longer only “which provider is fastest?” It is “what NHS-funded capacity is available for me through this provider, in my area, and what happens after assessment?”

---

Right to Choose has often been understood as the faster alternative to a long local ADHD or autism waiting list.

That assumption is becoming less reliable.

NHS North East and North Cumbria has now published unusually clear guidance explaining that eligible Right to Choose referrals remain open, but providers funded by the ICB are working to an approximately **78-week minimum waiting time for an initial assessment during 2026/27**.

The distinction matters. A patient may still have a legal right to choose an eligible NHS provider, while the amount of NHS-funded activity available through that provider is being managed locally.

For families and adults trying to decide what to do next, this creates a new problem: the waiting time shown on a provider’s website may not be the waiting time that applies to their NHS-funded referral.

This article explains what has changed, what has not, and what people should check before changing provider or assuming that a shorter advertised wait means faster NHS access.

## 1) What has actually changed in North East and North Cumbria?

The clearest new information is the number.

NHS North East and North Cumbria says that, for 2026/27, providers funded by the ICB are working to an approximately **78-week minimum waiting time for an initial ADHD or autism assessment**.

For someone referred recently, the ICB says this makes it unlikely that an assessment will take place during the current financial year, which ends on 31 March 2027.

But the ICB is equally clear about what this does **not** mean.

Eligible Right to Choose referrals have not been paused. Providers should continue to accept them and add patients to their waiting lists. Existing waits do not restart from zero. People who already have an assessment appointment would not routinely be expected to lose it because of the new arrangements, and assessments already underway should normally continue.

The 78-week approach is therefore not a closure of Right to Choose.

It is a change in how NHS-funded assessment activity is being scheduled.

### Nova take

The important distinction is between **the right to be referred** and **the speed at which funded assessment capacity is available**. Those are no longer the same question.

## 2) Why can a provider advertise a shorter wait?

This is probably the most confusing part for patients.

A Right to Choose provider may publish a waiting time on its website that is far shorter than 78 weeks. That does not necessarily mean the information is false.

The provider may genuinely have the clinical capacity to assess someone sooner. It may also operate different pathways: NHS-funded Right to Choose work, locally commissioned NHS work and private self-funded assessments.

North East and North Cumbria’s guidance says that a provider’s advertised waiting time does not necessarily represent the waiting time for an assessment funded by that ICB.

For 2026/27, the ICB has introduced **equalised waiting times** across the providers it funds, using the approximately 78-week minimum for initial assessment. It says the aim is to prioritise people who have already waited longest, reduce large differences between providers and use available funding consistently.

That creates a distinction that patients now need to understand:

**Provider capacity is not the same thing as NHS-funded capacity.**

A clinic may have an appointment available. The local NHS commissioner may not have funded enough activity for every eligible referral to use that appointment immediately.

### Nova take

The number on a provider website is becoming less useful on its own. Patients need to know whether that waiting time applies to their specific NHS-funded route.

## 3) Does this mean Right to Choose has stopped working?

No.

The NHS Choice Framework still sets out legal rights to choose a provider for many first outpatient referrals in England, including mental health referrals where the relevant requirements are met.

North East and North Cumbria explicitly says eligible Right to Choose referrals remain open.

What has changed is the practical value of choice as a shortcut around waiting.

Right to Choose gives a person a choice of eligible provider. It does not guarantee that the chosen provider will assess them within the timeframe advertised for another pathway or another area.

North East and North Cumbria also states that Right to Choose does not create a right to be assessed within a particular provider’s advertised timeframe.

This is an important correction to the way Right to Choose is often discussed online.

For several years, the public conversation has sometimes reduced the process to:

> local NHS wait = long<br>Right to Choose provider = shorter<br>therefore switching provider = faster

That can still be true in some circumstances.

It is no longer safe to assume it will be true everywhere.

## 4) Changing provider may not shorten the wait

A natural reaction to a 78-week minimum is to look for another provider.

North East and North Cumbria warns that changing Right to Choose provider will not necessarily result in an earlier assessment because the ICB has equalised the waiting-time arrangements across the providers it funds.

There is another practical issue.

The ICB advises people to consider the whole pathway before switching. Providers may differ in:

- ADHD medication and titration arrangements
- prescribing responsibility
- shared care
- follow-up
- whether assessment is online or face-to-face
- the age groups they assess
- what happens after the diagnostic decision

For ADHD in particular, a shorter diagnostic wait can lose some of its value if the person then enters a second long queue for medication or discovers that the provider’s NHS contract does not include the next stage they expected.

There is also a risk in cancelling one referral before fully understanding the replacement route. Local guidance in North East and North Cumbria says people should only be on one waiting list for the same assessment pathway at a time.

Before switching, patients should therefore establish exactly what will happen to their existing referral and whether time already spent waiting will carry over. In some provider changes, it may not.

“Can I switch?” and “Will switching help?” are different questions.

## 5) The 78-week figure is local — but the commissioning issue is wider

It would be misleading to describe 78 weeks as a new national Right to Choose waiting time.

It is not.

The figure published by North East and North Cumbria applies to the NHS-funded providers covered by that ICB’s 2026/27 arrangements.

But the underlying mechanism is appearing elsewhere.

NHS Hampshire and Isle of Wight has also published a September update explaining that it is using **Indicative Activity Plans**, or IAPs, across Right to Choose ADHD and autism providers.

An IAP sets out expected NHS-funded activity over a financial year. Hampshire and Isle of Wight says these plans are being used to manage demand, capacity and funding, and that current capacity constraints mean changing provider is unlikely to shorten the wait.

Its September update goes further: people waiting through Right to Choose will only be offered an assessment in 2027 if they meet prioritisation criteria, with the same approach applying to new referrals received during 2026/27.

The details are different from North East and North Cumbria. The two systems should not be treated as if they have introduced the same rule.

What they have in common is more important: **local commissioners are becoming more active in controlling the pace of NHS-funded Right to Choose activity.**

Hampshire and Isle of Wight states that the 2026/27 NHS Standard Contract provides the contractual basis for ICBs to set Indicative Activity Plans.

That helps explain why the experience of Right to Choose is becoming more geographically variable even though the underlying patient-choice framework remains national.

## 6) What happens if you are already waiting?

In North East and North Cumbria, the ICB says the 78-week arrangement does not reset a person’s waiting time.

If someone has already waited for a year, that year still counts.

Providers are being asked to prioritise people who have already been waiting longest, including people whose waiting time reaches approximately 78 weeks during the financial year.

The guidance also distinguishes initial assessment from later parts of the pathway.

If an ADHD assessment has already been completed and medication has been clinically recommended, the ICB says the 78-week minimum-wait approach for **initial assessment** should not create another 78-week wait for titration.

Where titration forms part of the provider’s NHS-funded pathway, the patient should progress according to that clinical pathway.

That is important because headlines about a “78-week wait” could easily be misunderstood as applying to every stage of care. The published guidance does not say that.

## 7) What should families and adults check now?

The most useful response is not to panic or immediately cancel an existing referral.

It is to get more precise information.

If you are considering Right to Choose, already waiting, or thinking about switching provider, ask:

- **Has the provider received and accepted my referral?**
- **What waiting time applies to an NHS-funded patient from my ICB?**
- **Is the wait shown on the provider’s website for NHS-funded Right to Choose patients, private patients, or both?**
- **Is my ICB using an Indicative Activity Plan or another activity limit with this provider?**
- **If I switch provider, do I lose the time I have already waited?**
- **What exactly is included in the assessment pathway?**
- **For ADHD, does the NHS-funded pathway include medication titration if medication is appropriate?**
- **Who remains responsible for prescribing if shared care is not agreed?**
- **What follow-up is available after assessment?**
- **Who should I contact if my needs become more urgent while I wait?**

For people in North East and North Cumbria, the ICB says individual waiting-list positions and appointments are held by the provider, so the provider is the correct place to ask about a specific referral.

If circumstances change significantly while waiting, the ICB advises speaking to a GP or other healthcare professional rather than simply waiting for the assessment date.

## 8) What this tells us about Right to Choose in 2026

The bigger change is not that Right to Choose has disappeared.

It is that **choice and capacity are separating**.

A patient may have the legal right to choose an eligible provider. The provider may have clinicians available. But the local NHS system may still plan how much funded activity can take place in that financial year.

That makes the pathway harder to understand.

It also makes transparency much more important.

If a provider advertises an eight-month wait but the relevant ICB is funding activity on a different timetable, patients need to know that before making a decision. If changing provider means joining a new queue, that should be clear before the original referral is withdrawn. If assessment is only one part of an ADHD pathway, patients need to understand who will provide titration and ongoing prescribing.

For Nova, the practical lesson is the same one that keeps emerging across the 2026 changes to ADHD and autism commissioning:

**access cannot be judged by the first appointment alone.**

A useful pathway needs to be understandable from referral through assessment, report, feedback and, where relevant, treatment and follow-up.

## FAQs

### Has Right to Choose been paused in North East and North Cumbria?

No. The ICB says eligible Right to Choose referrals remain open and providers should continue to accept them.

### Is there now a 78-week Right to Choose wait everywhere in England?

No. The approximately 78-week minimum applies to providers funded under North East and North Cumbria ICB’s 2026/27 arrangements. Other ICBs can have different arrangements.

### Why does my provider show a shorter waiting time?

The provider’s published wait may reflect its overall or private capacity and may not match the timetable for NHS-funded activity from your ICB.

### If I have already waited for a year, does my waiting time restart?

North East and North Cumbria says no. Waiting continues from the original referral date.

### Can I change Right to Choose provider to get seen sooner?

You can discuss changing provider with your GP, but in North East and North Cumbria the ICB says switching will not mean an earlier assessment under its equalised waiting-time arrangements. In other areas, the position may be different.

### Does the 78-week wait also apply to ADHD medication titration?

The published NENC guidance says the 78-week approach applies to initial assessment. If assessment is complete and titration is part of the provider’s NHS-funded pathway, it should progress according to that pathway rather than adding another automatic 78-week wait.

### What should I do while waiting?

Support should not depend entirely on having a diagnosis. For children, schools can consider SEND support and reasonable adjustments based on identified need. Adults can also ask for reasonable adjustments and support based on the difficulties they are experiencing. If needs or risk change, contact the relevant healthcare professional rather than waiting for the assessment.
