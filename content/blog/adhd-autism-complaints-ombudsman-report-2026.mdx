## Quick summary
- The Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman reviewed 3,000 complaints about ADHD and autism services from the past six years, with a closer analysis of 95 recent cases.
- Complaints rose from 410 in 2021/22 to 1,257 in 2025/26 — an increase of more than 200%.
- The report does not point to one single failure. It describes problems across referral, waiting, patient choice, assessment quality, recognition of diagnoses, medication, shared care and follow-up.
- Its central recommendation is stronger national commissioning guidance, alongside better evidence and clearer regulatory oversight.
- For patients, the practical lesson is that a successful pathway is not simply one that reaches a diagnosis. It also needs to connect that diagnosis to usable care and support.
---
Long waits remain one of the most visible problems in ADHD and autism care in England. But a new report from the Parliamentary and Health Service Ombudsman suggests that waiting time alone is too narrow a way to understand what is going wrong.
Published in August 2026, *Improving ADHD and autism services: commissioning with confidence* looks across thousands of complaints made about NHS-funded ADHD and autism services. What emerges is not one broken step, but a pathway that can become fragmented at several points: choosing a provider, reaching assessment, having a diagnosis recognised, accessing medication, moving into shared care, or finding support afterwards.
For families and adults trying to navigate these services, that distinction matters. A shorter assessment wait can be valuable. But it does not solve much if the next part of the pathway is unclear.

## 1) A 200% rise in complaints is a warning about the pathway — not a prevalence statistic
The headline number is striking.
The Ombudsman says it received 1,257 ADHD and autism-related complaints in 2025/26, compared with 410 in 2021/22. Across the last six years, it reviewed 3,000 complaints and then examined 95 recent complaints in greater detail.
Those figures should be interpreted carefully. Complaints data cannot tell us how common ADHD or autism is, and it cannot tell us that every patient has had the same experience. People who complain to the Ombudsman are, by definition, a particular group whose care has already gone sufficiently wrong for them to escalate the issue.
What the data can do is show repeated points of failure.
The Ombudsman describes problems affecting both children and adults, as well as pressure at the transition between children’s and adult services. It links the rise in complaints with increasing referrals, longer queues for assessment and treatment, and growing complexity in how services are commissioned and delivered.
That makes the complaint trend useful for a different reason: not as a measure of diagnosis, but as a stress test for the system.
### Nova take
The complaint numbers matter, but the pattern matters more: people are falling between different parts of the pathway.
## 2) The problem is not only waiting — it is fragmentation
It is tempting to reduce the current debate to a single number: how long someone waits for an assessment.
The Ombudsman’s casework shows why that misses part of the story.
People reported difficulty understanding patient choice and Right to Choose. Others encountered uncertainty about who was responsible for what after an assessment. Some struggled to access treatment or medication after receiving an ADHD diagnosis. There were also examples of poor communication between providers and NHS organisations.
These are different problems, but they share a common feature: the pathway is split across organisations that may not join together cleanly.
A person might be referred by a GP, assessed by one provider, need titration from another service, eventually move towards shared care with primary care, and rely on a separate local service for ongoing support. For a child, school and local SEND provision may also form an important part of what happens next.
Each individual component can make sense on paper. The difficulty appears when responsibility moves from one to another.
NHS England’s 2026/27 payment guidance recognises this explicitly. It says commissioners should ensure robust shared-care arrangements and clear handovers where a provider cannot deliver every part of the pathway.
That is significant because it shifts the definition of a good service away from “assessment completed” towards “pathway completed safely and coherently”.
### Nova take
A fast assessment is less useful if the patient then enters another queue with no clear handover.
## 3) A diagnosis can be clinically valid and still fail to become usable care
One of the most important themes in the report concerns what happens after diagnosis.
The Ombudsman describes inconsistency in how diagnoses obtained through NHS-funded choice pathways are recognised by other parts of the health system. It also highlights difficulties accessing ADHD medication, titration and shared care.
This is not a minor administrative issue.
For someone seeking ADHD assessment, diagnosis may be followed by a discussion about treatment. If medication is appropriate and wanted, titration usually requires specialist oversight before any shared-care arrangement with a GP can be considered. If there is ambiguity about who is responsible for that stage, the patient can have an answer without a functioning treatment pathway.
The same broader principle applies to autism. Diagnosis may provide clarity, but people may still need reasonable adjustments, practical recommendations, school or workplace support, or help understanding what the diagnosis means in day-to-day life.
NHS England’s current payment guidance says unnecessary reassessment should be avoided and that diagnostic reports from independent providers eligible under Right to Choose should be accepted by NHS-commissioned titration services unless there is a clear clinical reason not to.
That does not mean every report must automatically be accepted in every circumstance. It does show that national policy is now directly addressing the disruption caused when one part of the system does not trust or recognise another without a clinical reason.
### Nova take
A diagnosis is not a successful pathway if treatment, recognition or support breaks down immediately afterwards.
## 4) The expanding provider market has exposed a quality and oversight question
The Ombudsman also raises a more difficult issue: how quality is assured as the market for ADHD and autism assessment has expanded.
Its report identifies variation in assessment structure and quality, and notes that organisations do not always interpret existing guidance in the same way. It also points to gaps in oversight for some diagnostic-only services.
The report therefore recommends that government consider whether ADHD and autism diagnostic activity should fall more clearly within Care Quality Commission registration and inspection requirements, including where a provider offers diagnosis without ongoing treatment.
That recommendation should not be read as evidence that independent or online assessment is inherently poor. The report itself is concerned with the opposite problem: a system in which people can encounter different standards and different levels of accountability depending on where their pathway takes place.
Remote assessment is also not the issue by itself. High-quality assessment can involve remote elements where clinically appropriate. The more important questions are whether the pathway gathers sufficient evidence, uses appropriately qualified professionals, considers differential and co-occurring conditions, and produces a clinically useful report.
For autism, that generally means a properly multidisciplinary approach. For ADHD, NICE expects a full clinical and psychosocial assessment rather than diagnosis based on a questionnaire or single score alone.
### Nova take
More provider choice only works well when patients and commissioners can also see what good quality looks like.
## 5) The Ombudsman is asking for national commissioning rules, not one national provider
The report’s first recommendation is for new national commissioning guidance and an implementation framework for ADHD and autism.
That may sound bureaucratic, but the underlying problem is practical.
Integrated care boards currently have to make local decisions about capacity, contracts, patient choice, quality, payment and how different services connect. When demand is rising and the provider landscape is becoming more complex, local variation can become very large.
The Ombudsman argues that national guidance should clarify responsibilities and help ICBs commission more joined-up pathways. It also wants better use of evidence around digital diagnostic technologies and asks government to consider stronger regulatory oversight.
This does not necessarily mean every area would run an identical service. Local population needs and service models will still differ.
The more realistic aim is consistency around the fundamentals: who is responsible, what a pathway should contain, how quality is assured, how patients use choice, and what happens after diagnosis.
That direction is consistent with NHS England’s 2026/27 payment work, which has already begun to define common assessment currencies, guide prices and commissioning expectations.
### Nova take
The emerging policy direction is less about creating one pathway everywhere and more about setting clearer rules for what every pathway should reliably deliver.
## 6) What families and adults can ask now
The Ombudsman’s recommendations will take time to translate into national policy. People currently waiting for an assessment still have to navigate the system as it exists today.
Useful questions are therefore practical rather than abstract:
- Who is responsible for each stage of the pathway?
- Does the provider offer assessment only, or assessment plus treatment or follow-up?
- If ADHD medication is being considered, who provides titration?
- If shared care is relevant, how is the handover to primary care expected to work?
- What happens if another NHS service has questions about the diagnostic report?
- What support is available while waiting?
- What does the final report include, and who receives it?
- For a child, how will school information be gathered and how can recommendations be used afterwards?
- If care is delayed or responsibilities are unclear, who should the patient contact first?
These questions cannot remove a waiting list. They can, however, expose gaps before a patient reaches them.

## What this means in practice
The Ombudsman report adds an important layer to the current ADHD and autism debate.
England clearly has a capacity problem. But expanding capacity alone will not solve fragmented care. A system can conduct more assessments and still leave people stranded between diagnosis, treatment and support.
Three things now look increasingly important:
- **clearer national commissioning expectations**, so local systems do not have to invent the fundamentals independently
- **better continuity across providers**, particularly where assessment, titration and ongoing care are delivered by different organisations
- **visible quality standards and accountability**, so expanding access does not create more uncertainty about what patients will actually receive
For patients, the most useful measure of a pathway is therefore not only “How quickly can I be assessed?”
It is also: “What happens to me after the assessment is finished?”
## FAQs
### Have ADHD and autism complaints to the Ombudsman really tripled?
The Ombudsman received 1,257 complaints in 2025/26 compared with 410 in 2021/22, an increase of more than 200%. That reflects complaints reaching the Ombudsman, not the total number of patients or diagnoses.
### Does the report say private or online assessments are unreliable?
No. It identifies variation in quality and oversight across a growing provider market and recommends clearer national standards and regulation. The relevant question is the quality and completeness of an individual pathway, not simply whether it is delivered remotely or independently.
### Does an NHS-funded Right to Choose diagnosis have to be accepted?
NHS England’s 2026/27 payment guidance says reports from independent providers, including providers eligible under Right to Choose, should be accepted by NHS-commissioned titration services unless there is a clear clinical reason not to. Individual clinical circumstances can still matter.
### What is the Ombudsman asking the government to change?
Its recommendations include national commissioning guidance and an implementation framework, stronger evidence around digital diagnostic technology, and consideration of changes to regulatory requirements for ADHD and autism diagnostic providers.
### Why does this matter if someone is already on a waiting list?
Because the report shows that reaching assessment is only one stage. Knowing who is responsible for treatment, shared care, follow-up and support can reduce uncertainty later in the pathway.
