## Quick summary
- September 2026 marks the start of **Experts at Hand**, a new offer designed to bring professionals such as educational psychologists, speech and language therapists and occupational therapists closer to mainstream education settings.
- Children do not need a diagnosis to benefit from the offer; support is intended to be based on identified need.
- Schools are also receiving the new **Inclusive Mainstream Fund**, worth over £500 million a year across mainstream settings, including £400 million a year for schools.
- Families should not expect a new individual specialist service to appear everywhere immediately. Experts at Hand is being phased in locally and its first year focuses heavily on group and whole-class approaches.
- EHCP rights have **not** been replaced this September. The current statutory system remains in place, and changes to support received through EHCPs are not expected before at least September 2030.
---
The start of a new school year often brings fresh promises about SEND support. September 2026 is different in one important respect: parts of the government’s reform programme are now moving from consultation documents into actual delivery.
Two changes matter particularly for families.
The first is **Experts at Hand**, which local areas are expected to begin introducing from September. The second is the **Inclusive Mainstream Fund**, new money intended to help mainstream schools build support into everyday teaching rather than waiting for a diagnosis or statutory process.
Both fit the same policy direction: identify needs earlier, bring specialist expertise closer to schools, and reduce the extent to which children must cross formal thresholds before anything changes.
That is an important ambition.
But it is equally important to be clear about what has *not* changed. The new offer is phased. It will look different between areas. And current EHCP rights and duties remain in place.
For parents of autistic children, children with ADHD, or children whose needs are still being explored, September is therefore a moment for informed questions rather than assumptions.

## 1) What actually starts this September: Experts at Hand
Experts at Hand is one of the most concrete parts of the government’s SEND reform programme to begin now.
The model is intended to bring health and education professionals into mainstream nurseries, schools, colleges and other settings, working alongside existing staff rather than requiring every child to enter a separate specialist pathway first.
The government lists professionals such as:
- speech and language therapists
- occupational therapists
- educational psychologists
- specialist teachers
Support can include observing children in their usual learning environment, helping staff identify needs, developing strategies, training and coaching school staff, small-group support and, where appropriate, some individual input.
The central principle is that a child does **not** need a diagnosis to access the support. Need is supposed to come first.
That matters for autism and ADHD because assessment waits can be long. If a child is struggling with communication, attention, sensory regulation, transitions or participation, waiting for a diagnostic outcome before changing the environment can allow difficulties to escalate unnecessarily.
Experts at Hand is backed by £1.8 billion over three years, but local authorities and ICBs are responsible for designing the offer in their area.
That last detail is important.

September matters because parts of SEND reform are finally moving from policy into delivery — but delivery will still be local.
## 2) The first year will not look like an individual therapy service for every child
The name “Experts at Hand” can create an expectation that every child with additional needs will suddenly have direct one-to-one access to a specialist.
That is not how the first year is being described.
DfE guidance says local areas will introduce the offer in phases and prioritise services according to local need. In year one, support is expected to focus mainly on group and whole-class approaches so that expertise can reach more children.
That might mean a speech and language therapist helping a school improve communication strategies across a class. It could involve an educational psychologist coaching staff around attention, regulation or classroom routines. It might mean specialist teachers supporting inclusive practice rather than taking one child out of class for a separate programme.
There can still be individual input where needed, but families should not assume the new funding creates an automatic entitlement to a particular professional or number of sessions.
This is where transparency will matter.
A needs-led system only becomes useful when parents know what exists locally, how a school accesses it, what criteria are being used, and what happens when universal or group support is not enough.
### Nova take
Support without diagnosis is only meaningful if families can see what the support is and how to reach it.
## 3) Schools also have new money to make mainstream support more inclusive
Experts at Hand is only one part of the September picture.
The Inclusive Mainstream Fund is worth more than £500 million per year across mainstream settings for three years. For state-funded mainstream schools, £400 million per year is allocated to help strengthen inclusive practice.
The stated aim is important: schools should be better able to identify and support commonly occurring needs earlier, without requiring a diagnosis or statutory process first.
DfE guidance allows schools to use the funding to build a stronger core offer, including:
- high-quality adaptive teaching
- early evidence-based targeted support
- inclusive classroom environments
- staff development
- approaches that remove common barriers to participation and learning
This is less visible than creating a new assessment service. A parent may not be told, “your child has received Inclusive Mainstream Fund support”.
The effect may instead appear in how the school organises teaching, trains staff, manages sensory or communication needs, or introduces targeted group interventions.
That makes it harder to judge from the outside, but not less important.
For years, one of the tensions in SEND policy has been that support is supposed to be needs-led while families often feel they need formal evidence before meaningful adjustments happen.
The new fund is explicitly designed to push resources further upstream.
### Nova take
The test is not whether a school can name the new fund. It is whether support becomes available earlier in the classroom.
## 4) EHCP rights have not changed this September
This is probably the most important clarification for families.
Experts at Hand does **not** replace an Education, Health and Care Plan.
DfE guidance says the new offer is additional to existing statutory support and should never be used to replace provision already specified in an EHCP.
More broadly, the government’s proposed reformed SEND system requires legislation and is not expected to come into effect until September 2029. The House of Commons Library notes that the current system remains in place until then, and that no changes to support received through EHCPs are expected before at least September 2030.
So, in September 2026:
- parents can still request an EHC needs assessment
- local authorities still have their current statutory duties
- existing EHCP provision remains legally relevant
- schools still have duties under the current SEND framework
- Experts at Hand is additional support, not a substitute for statutory provision
This distinction matters because the reform debate has already created anxiety about whether families should rush to obtain an EHCP before the system changes.
The current position is more measured: significant reform is proposed, but the statutory architecture has not disappeared this term.
### Nova take
The biggest misunderstanding right now is that EHCP rights have already changed. They have not.
## 5) Why the timing feels so sensitive
The new support is arriving while demand for statutory SEND provision continues to rise.
Official DfE data shows 538,547 pupils in schools in England had an EHC plan in 2025/26 — 6.0% of pupils and 11.6% more than the previous year.
That sits alongside a much larger group receiving SEN support without an EHC plan.
These numbers help explain the logic behind reform. A system in which more and more support pressure concentrates around statutory plans becomes difficult for families, schools and local authorities alike.
But they also explain why families can be wary of a shift towards earlier mainstream provision.
If “earlier support” means more specialist expertise, better teaching and meaningful adjustments before crisis, it could reduce the need to fight for formal escalation.
If it means asking schools to carry more responsibility without enough capacity, families may experience the reform very differently.
The funding and specialist teams are intended to prevent the second outcome. September is the beginning of the test, not proof that the model has succeeded.
### Nova take
Earlier support is a good principle. What matters now is whether the resource arrives with the responsibility.
## 6) What parents can ask their school or SENCO this term
Families do not need to know every detail of SEND financing to ask useful questions.
If your child already has identified needs, or you are concerned about how they are managing in school, useful questions include:
- What is our local Experts at Hand offer?
- Has the school been told which specialists it can access?
- Which areas of need are being prioritised locally this year?
- How will staff decide which children benefit from targeted or group support?
- What changes is the school making through its Inclusive Mainstream Fund allocation?
- How will parents be involved in planning support?
- How will progress be reviewed?
- What happens if group or universal support is not enough?
- If my child has an EHCP, how does this sit alongside the provision already specified?
- If my child does not have a diagnosis, what support can be put in place now?
The answer may not yet be fully developed in every area. The programme is deliberately phased.
But “it is still being rolled out” should not become the same thing as “nothing can be done until diagnosis”.
## 7) What this means for autism and ADHD assessment
Earlier school support should not be framed as an alternative to appropriate diagnostic assessment.
The two answer different questions.
Support asks: **what does this child need in order to participate, learn and feel safe now?**
Assessment asks: **does the overall pattern of development, behaviour and functioning meet criteria for a particular neurodevelopmental condition, and what formulation best explains the child’s presentation?**
A child can need classroom adjustments before anyone knows whether they are autistic or have ADHD. Equally, a diagnostic assessment can still be valuable for understanding a child’s profile, informing future support and giving families clearer language for what they are seeing.
In a good system, those processes should complement each other.
School evidence also remains important within child autism and ADHD assessments. Observations about attention, communication, sensory needs, peer relationships, transitions, organisation, regulation and masking can help clinicians understand how a child presents across settings.
If Experts at Hand leads to more informed observation and earlier support in schools, it may therefore improve the quality of information around a child as well as the help they receive before diagnosis.

## What this means in practice
September 2026 should not be described as the moment England’s SEND system was replaced.
It is better understood as the beginning of a transition.
Some of the supporting infrastructure is starting now:
- new mainstream inclusion funding
- local Experts at Hand teams
- closer working between education and health professionals
- a stronger policy expectation that support can begin without diagnosis
The larger statutory reform comes later and still requires legislation.
For families, the immediate opportunity is to ask what the new investment means in their own school and local area.
For schools, the challenge is to turn broad concepts such as “inclusive by design” into visible changes in classrooms.
And for policymakers, the test is whether earlier support genuinely reduces escalation — or simply creates a new layer of expectation around a system that remains stretched.
## FAQs
### Does my child need an autism or ADHD diagnosis to access Experts at Hand?
No. DfE guidance says support is based on need and a diagnosis is not required.
### Is Experts at Hand available everywhere from September 2026?
Local areas are expected to begin introducing it from September, but rollout is phased and priorities will differ between areas. Families should ask their school, local authority or local parent carer forum about the offer locally.
### Does Experts at Hand replace an EHCP?
No. It is additional to existing statutory support and should not replace provision specified in an EHCP.
### Have EHCP rules changed this September?
No. The current statutory system remains in place. The proposed wider reformed system is not expected to begin until 2029, and changes to support received through existing EHCPs are not expected before at least September 2030.
### What is the Inclusive Mainstream Fund?
It is new funding for mainstream settings intended to strengthen inclusive practice and provide earlier support. More than £500 million per year is being provided across mainstream settings, including £400 million per year for schools.
### Should families still pursue assessment if school can provide support without diagnosis?
That depends on the child and the purpose of assessment. Support should not be withheld while a child waits, but diagnosis can still provide important clinical clarity and inform longer-term planning.
