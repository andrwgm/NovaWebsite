ADHD and recurring digestive problems can feel like two separate things to explain. Research highlighted by the University of Warwick on 11 September 2026 brings them into the same conversation.

For adults with ADHD and parents reading the headlines, the useful question is what kind of connection the researchers have found. Does the evidence point to how the gut and brain communicate, the effects of medication, everyday eating patterns, or some combination of these?

## What did the review find?

Warwick describes a systematic review and meta-analysis covering **23 studies and more than 1.9 million people**, aged 2–65, across several countries. Its summary reports higher levels of gastrointestinal symptoms among people with ADHD, including IBS, nearly twice the odds of constipation and more than four times the odds of encopresis, or bowel soiling. These are findings reported in the [university's research announcement](https://warwick.ac.uk/news/pressreleases/people-with-adhd-more-likely-to-suffer-gut-problems/).

The distinction between a review and a new experiment matters. The researchers brought existing studies together; they did not recruit 1.9 million people into one new trial. Combining evidence can help identify a pattern across studies, but it does not automatically explain how that pattern arises.

Nor does “twice the odds” mean that half of people with ADHD have constipation. Odds compare the number of people with an outcome against those without it. A relative comparison needs a starting frequency before it can be translated into an absolute chance. The headline figures alone cannot tell an individual how likely they are to develop a particular symptom.

## What does the gut–brain connection actually mean?

The digestive system has its own network of nerves, known as the enteric nervous system, which helps control digestion and communicates with the brain.

> The connection works in both directions: the brain can influence digestive function, and signals from the digestive system can influence the central nervous system.

[Johns Hopkins Medicine explains this two-way communication](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/wellness-and-prevention/the-brain-gut-connection).

That helps make sense of why researchers investigate digestive symptoms alongside conditions usually discussed in terms of attention, behaviour or emotions. It gives them a biological framework in which to ask questions. It does not, by itself, establish the answer for ADHD.

IBS offers a useful example of what gut–brain interaction means in practice. The US National Institute of Diabetes and Digestive and Kidney Diseases describes IBS as a disorder of gut–brain interaction. Changes in gut sensitivity and bowel muscle contractions can contribute to pain, bloating, constipation or diarrhoea. Symptoms can occur without visible damage to the digestive tract. [Read the NIDDK explanation of IBS](https://www.niddk.nih.gov/health-information/digestive-diseases/irritable-bowel-syndrome/definition-facts).

This is a physical account of how symptoms can happen. It should not be read as saying that discomfort is imagined. It also does not mean that everybody with constipation has IBS, or that the same mechanism explains every digestive problem associated with ADHD.

## What might explain the association with ADHD?

Warwick outlines several possibilities: differences in gut bacteria and signalling through the vagus nerve, ADHD-related traits and irregular eating patterns, and medication effects. The researchers have not established which factors explain the association. [Source: Warwick](https://warwick.ac.uk/news/pressreleases/people-with-adhd-more-likely-to-suffer-gut-problems/).

### Gut bacteria and communication with the brain

The microbiota are the microorganisms living in the gut. Johns Hopkins describes interactions between gut bacteria, hormones and nerve signals as an area of research into communication between the digestive system and the rest of the body. [Source: Johns Hopkins Medicine](https://www.hopkinsmedicine.org/health/wellness-and-prevention/the-brain-gut-connection).

There are several separate questions here. Are there differences between groups? Do those differences precede symptoms? Do they contribute to symptoms? And would changing them improve anyone's health?

Evidence that answers the first question cannot simply be carried across to answer the others. A comparison between groups is not a test of a probiotic, a diet or a commercial microbiome service.

### Everyday patterns as a possible contributor

An explanation involving eating patterns would raise a different research question from an explanation involving gut signalling. Researchers would need to examine whether those patterns account for any of the association, and how they interact with other factors.

This possibility should not become an assumption about an individual. Finding an association in a population does not establish what someone eats, how they organise their day or why their symptoms began. It certainly provides no basis for blaming a child or adult for having digestive difficulties.

### Medication as part of the picture

There is a concrete reason to examine treatment separately. The NHS lists stomach ache, reduced appetite, nausea and vomiting among possible side effects of methylphenidate in adults. Not everyone experiences them. [Source: NHS methylphenidate guidance](https://www.nhs.uk/medicines/methylphenidate-adults/side-effects-of-methylphenidate-for-adults/).

For research, that creates a question: how much of any observed difference relates to ADHD, and how much might relate to treatment or other differences between the groups? Simply comparing people with and without a diagnosis cannot settle that on its own.

For someone taking medication, the NHS advises telling their doctor if side effects are troublesome or persistent while continuing their prescribed medicine. A research headline is not a reason to alter treatment independently.

## How much can this research tell us?

A large combined sample is useful, but size is only one part of judging evidence. To assess a meta-analysis properly, we would also want to know how ADHD and digestive symptoms were identified, whether the studies produced similar results, and how medication and other possible explanations were handled.

Those are questions for the full methods and results, rather than details that can be inferred from a press release. The same applies to age: a review that includes both children and adults does not establish that the association is equally strong at every age.

Our reading is that the research makes the connection worth investigating more carefully. It leaves open the possibility of several contributing pathways rather than one explanation that fits everyone.

## What is the useful takeaway?

Warwick's researchers suggest that clinicians ask about digestive symptoms during ADHD consultations and consider their effect on quality of life. They explicitly caution that the findings do not prove ADHD causes gut problems. [Source: Warwick](https://warwick.ac.uk/news/pressreleases/people-with-adhd-more-likely-to-suffer-gut-problems/).

For Nova, the editorial takeaway is that these concerns belong in the same conversation while still deserving individual assessment.

> A possible connection can help frame better questions; it should not close down the search for an explanation.

If you recognise both experiences, mention both when discussing your health. You do not need to arrive with a theory about your gut bacteria. The research is useful because it invites closer attention to a connection that may matter in daily life, even while its causes remain unresolved.
