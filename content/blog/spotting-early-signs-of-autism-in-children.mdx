## Introduction

If you are reading this, you are probably doing what many thoughtful parents do: noticing patterns, comparing stories, and trying to understand your child with as much care as possible. This article is not a checklist for diagnosing autism. It is a gentle map of **common early differences** that families sometimes report, alongside reminders about why context matters.

Autism is a spectrum. Two children can share a diagnosis and still look very different day to day. What matters most is whether a child’s profile is impacting wellbeing, learning, relationships, or participation—and whether support could help.

## Recognising early signs of autism

Many families first seek answers when they notice differences in **social communication** and **flexibility of play**. That does not automatically mean autism, but it can be a helpful starting point for a conversation with a qualified clinician.

### Social communication differences

Some children show a thoughtful, quiet style of engaging with others. You might notice:

- Limited use of eye contact, or eye contact that is brief and functional rather than sustained
- A reduced tendency to point to share interest (“look at that!”), even when needs are communicated clearly
- Differences in gesture use, facial expression variety, or reciprocal back-and-forth babbling

### Play and imagination

Play can be joyful and also highly structured. Families sometimes describe:

- Strong preference for sorting, lining up, or repeating sequences
- Creative play that is detailed, but less reliant on “pretend roles”
- Deep, enthusiastic knowledge of a favourite topic

### Sensory processing

Sensory experiences are not “good” or “bad”—they are personal. Some children seek movement, deep pressure, or visual patterns. Others become overwhelmed by noise, crowds, or certain textures. What helps is **accommodation**, not pressure to tolerate discomfort.

## When to seek a professional opinion

If you feel stuck between “wait and see” and “something feels important,” you are not alone. A good assessment should feel respectful, developmental, and grounded in your child’s real life—not a rushed label.

## A note on language and stigma

Children deserve language that protects dignity. The goal of early identification is not to narrow a child’s story—it is to open doors: understanding, adaptations, school support, and community connection.
